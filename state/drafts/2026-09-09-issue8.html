COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

The United States and Iran Trade Fire Across the Persian Gulf

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Security

On September 5, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at a United States aircraft carrier and a guided missile destroyer operating near the Strait of Hormuz. United States Central Command said the warships evaded the missiles and that no American personnel were harmed. In response, United States forces struck three Iranian oil tankers, disabling two near the Iranian coast off Kharg Island and Jask and destroying a third in the Gulf of Oman. The exchange was one of the sharpest escalations in a months long confrontation that has increasingly moved onto the water, with commercial shipping repeatedly caught in the crossfire. It matters because the world's most important oil chokepoint, through which roughly a fifth of globally traded crude passes, has become the setting for direct military exchanges between the two powers.

One Perspective

Washington describes its actions as defensive and proportionate. United States officials say Iranian forces launched unprovoked attacks on Navy ships in international waters, and that striking the tankers, which they link to sanctioned oil exports that help fund Tehran's military, was a measured way to impose costs without hitting Iranian territory or killing Iranian personnel. In this account, freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz is a global public good, and the American naval presence is meant to deter Iran from threatening or closing the passage.

Another Perspective

Tehran casts the confrontation as resistance to an American campaign of pressure and blockade against its shipping. Iranian officials argue that interdiction of their oil exports amounts to economic warfare and a violation of their sovereignty, and that the missile launches were a warning against further strikes rather than an opening blow. Iran's parliament speaker has threatened retaliation against energy infrastructure across the Gulf, including United States interests, if attacks continue, presenting any wider escalation as a consequence of Washington's choices.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the confrontation has settled into a tit for tat pattern at sea that neither side seems eager to turn into a full air or ground war, yet one that carries a high risk of miscalculation. The Strait of Hormuz is narrow, crowded, and central to global energy markets, so even a limited incident can move oil prices and pull in Gulf states and outside navies. The tanker war between the United States and Iran in the 1980s offers a precedent, showing that maritime confrontations can grind on for months and occasionally spike into larger clashes. Insurers, shippers, and neighboring governments are now watching whether the current pattern holds or tips toward wider conflict.

POLICY · EUROPE

Britain Bans Trade With Israeli Settlements as Allies Follow

Est. read time: 4 min · Europe · Trade

On September 8, British Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband announced that the United Kingdom will ban imports of goods produced in Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank and sanction people and companies that work to expand them, covering services such as construction, financing, real estate, and advertising. The measures are expected to take six to nine months to come into force, and they arrived alongside parallel steps by Canada and France, with Paris pressing for a European Union wide ban. Israel responded by announcing the closure of the British consulate in Jerusalem, and the United States said it would not follow London's lead. The step marks a notable hardening of Western policy toward settlements that most of the international community regards as illegal under international law.

One Perspective

Supporters argue that the ban puts legal and economic weight behind a long standing position that settlements built on occupied land are unlawful and an obstacle to a two state solution. They contend that trading with settlement enterprises effectively helps finance their growth, and that coordinated action by several governments raises the cost of policies they see as entrenching occupation and displacing Palestinians. From this vantage point, the move is an overdue attempt to enforce international law rather than a purely symbolic gesture.

Another Perspective

The Israeli government rejects the measures as hostile and discriminatory, arguing that they single out Israel and prejudge questions that should be resolved through negotiation, and it framed the consulate closure as an answer to a government it accuses of working against it. Critics in Washington and elsewhere warn the bans could harden positions, complicate efforts to stabilize Gaza, and strain the Western alliance, with the United States secretary of state signaling that Washington views the approach as counterproductive. In this reading, unilateral economic penalties reduce leverage rather than advance a durable settlement.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts point out that settlement goods make up a very small share of trade, so the immediate economic impact is limited and the significance is mainly political and legal. The coordinated nature of the announcements, and France's push for a bloc wide measure, suggests a broader Western willingness to turn rhetoric about settlement illegality into concrete policy, which could widen the gap with Washington. Legal scholars point to advisory opinions and long standing United Nations positions holding the settlements unlawful, while noting that enforcement has historically been weak. The test will be whether more states join, and whether the measures change Israeli policy or mainly rearrange diplomatic alignments.

DIPLOMACY · EAST ASIA

Xi Jinping Courts the World Before His Washington Summit

Est. read time: 3 min · East Asia · Diplomacy

In the weeks before a planned state visit to Washington, Chinese leader Xi Jinping has embarked on an unusually dense run of diplomacy, combining state visits with appearances at multilateral summits and a meeting with Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi, as Beijing works to project influence ahead of talks with President Donald Trump. The White House visit, expected later in September at Trump's invitation, would be the first such trip in years and is reported to include a large Chinese business delegation. The agenda is expected to span trade, investment, artificial intelligence, the conflict involving Iran, Taiwan, and nuclear arms control, the last made more pressing by the lapse of the New START treaty between the United States and Russia earlier in 2026. It matters as the most direct high level engagement between the world's two largest economies in a period of intense strategic rivalry.

One Perspective

Those who see leverage in the approach read the diplomatic tour as a calculated effort to enter the Washington talks from a position of strength. By courting partners across Asia and the Global South and showcasing economic ties, Beijing signals that it has options and is not isolated, which its officials argue improves its bargaining position on tariffs, technology controls, and Taiwan. In this framing, engaging Washington is pragmatic, aimed at stabilizing a volatile relationship while protecting what China calls its core interests.

Another Perspective

Skeptics in the United States and among some allies caution that summitry can paper over deep and unresolved disputes, and that a high profile visit risks conferring prestige without changing Chinese conduct on trade practices, technology transfer, or pressure on Taiwan. They argue that Beijing uses parallel diplomacy with rivals of the West to blunt pressure, and that enforceable commitments, not favorable atmospherics, should be the measure of any deal. From this view the meeting is worth holding, but should be judged by concrete and verifiable outcomes.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the moment as an attempt to manage rather than resolve a structural rivalry. The lapse of New START leaves the two largest nuclear powers without formal arms control for the first time in decades, and China's own growing arsenal complicates any future framework, giving nuclear governance fresh prominence. On trade and technology the two economies remain deeply intertwined even as each works to reduce its dependence on the other. Precedent from earlier United States and China summits suggests such meetings can lower the temperature and yield narrow agreements, but rarely settle the underlying contest over technology, security, and influence.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why is the Strait of Hormuz so important to the global economy?

A: The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway linking the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, and roughly a fifth of the world's traded oil, along with a large share of its liquefied natural gas, passes through it. Because there are few practical alternative routes for much of that supply, any disruption or threat to shipping there can push up global energy prices and ripple through markets far from the Gulf. That concentration of traffic is what turns even limited military incidents in the strait into a matter of worldwide concern.

Q: What does it mean when goods are called illegal under international law, as with Israeli settlements?

A: Most governments and international bodies hold that settlements built by an occupying power on territory captured in war are prohibited under the Fourth Geneva Convention, which restricts moving a civilian population into occupied land. Describing settlement goods as unlawful reflects that legal position, though enforcement is left to individual states and international institutions rather than a single global authority. Israel disputes this interpretation, arguing that the territory's status is unresolved and should be decided through negotiation.

Q: Why does the expiration of an arms control treaty like New START matter?

A: New START limited the number of deployed long range nuclear weapons held by the United States and Russia and let each side verify the other's arsenal through inspections and data exchanges. Without it, those formal limits and verification measures lapse, leaving the two largest nuclear powers with less insight into each other's forces for the first time in decades. Analysts worry that the absence of such guardrails raises the risk of miscalculation and a renewed buildup, especially as other nuclear armed states expand their own stockpiles.
