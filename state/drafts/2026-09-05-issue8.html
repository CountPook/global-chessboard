COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

The SCO Turns Twenty Five and Presses for a Multipolar Order

Est. read time: 4 min · Central Asia · Multilateralism

On August 31 and September 1 the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation held its heads of state summit in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan, marking the bloc's twenty fifth anniversary. Leaders including China's Xi Jinping, Russia's Vladimir Putin, India's Narendra Modi, and Iran's Masoud Pezeshkian adopted the Bishkek Declaration alongside more than two dozen additional documents, and approved regulations for a new center to coordinate responses to security threats among members. The declaration criticized the growing use of unilateral sanctions and coercive economic measures and called for a more equitable world order, and Kyrgyzstan handed the rotating chairmanship to Pakistan. The summit matters because it gathered states representing more than a third of the world's population and a large share of its energy at a moment of deep friction with the United States and its partners.

One Perspective

Supporters describe the SCO as the scaffolding of a genuine multipolar order. By drawing China, Russia, India, Iran, and the Central Asian states into one forum, they argue, it offers an alternative to Western led institutions and a venue where the Global South can set terms rather than accept them. From this view the declaration's language against sanctions speaks for a large part of the world that sees coercive measures as a tool wielded mainly by Washington and Brussels, and the new security center is evidence that the group is slowly building institutional depth rather than merely holding annual photo opportunities.

Another Perspective

Skeptics counter that the SCO remains a loose grouping of states with divergent and sometimes rival interests. India and China still contest a disputed border, India and Pakistan are open adversaries, and the members compete as often as they cooperate. On this reading the consensus documents are declaratory rather than binding, with no mutual defense obligation, no joint command, and limited economic integration. The condemnation of sanctions, critics note, has not stopped members from trading around each other's interests, which suggests the summit produced more rhetoric than commitment.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts tend to see the SCO as a forum that has grown in membership and symbolic weight while remaining far short of an alliance. Internal rivalries cap how far integration can go, and the bloc has no machinery to compel members to act together. Yet the optics still carry meaning, and a brief, widely noted exchange between Modi and Xi was read as a sign of a cautious thaw after their 2020 border clash. The organization's real influence lies less in hard commitments than in normalizing an alternative diplomatic space, one that lets its members hedge against Washington without formally breaking with it.

SECURITY · SAHEL

Niger's Military Government Says It Repelled a Coup Attempt

Est. read time: 3 min · West Africa · Security

In the early hours of August 29 groups of soldiers attacked several sites in Niger's capital, Niamey, including the presidential palace, an air base, and the state broadcaster, in what the military government led by General Abdourahamane Tiani later described as a foiled coup attempt. Loyalist Presidential Guard units regained control over the course of the day, reportedly with support from Russian Africa Corps personnel and drones, and the authorities said they arrested around a hundred people. Reports put the death toll at more than two dozen. The episode matters as a sign of strain inside a government that seized power in a 2023 coup and has leaned heavily on Moscow for its security.

One Perspective

The government casts the events as an attempted power grab by a faction of mutinous officers, driven by personal ambition and, in its telling, encouraged from outside. It presents the failure of the plot as proof of the junta's stability and popular backing, and it credits security partnerships with Russia and Algeria for helping defeat the assault. In this framing the state met a violent challenge and prevailed, and the arrests that followed are the lawful response of a government defending itself.

Another Perspective

Critics and some regional analysts read the mutiny instead as a symptom of deep discontent within the armed forces. They point to worsening insecurity from jihadist violence, complaints of favoritism in promotions, and unease over the growing presence of foreign fighters as sources of anger among rank and file soldiers. On this account a government that rests on force rather than consent is inherently brittle, and its dependence on outside troops to survive an internal revolt reveals fragility rather than strength.

The Strategic Reality

Niger sits within a Sahel belt where military governments in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger have pushed out former Western partners and turned to Russia while jihadist violence has spread. The survival of Tiani's government with Russian help underscores how central Moscow has become to security in parts of Africa, and how much these juntas now rely on it. Analysts note that seizing power has proved far easier than governing, and that none of the region's coups has yet reversed the insecurity that helped bring the soldiers to power in the first place.

ECONOMICS · EAST ASIA

Japan's Bond Yields Hit a Thirty Year High as Cheap Money Fades

Est. read time: 3 min · Japan · Markets

On September 1 the yield on Japan's benchmark ten year government bond touched 3 percent for the first time since 1996, the clearest sign yet that the era of ultra low interest rates that long defined one of the world's largest economies is drawing to a close. Yields have climbed across the curve as investors bet that the Bank of Japan will keep raising rates and weigh the fiscal plans of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government, which has approved a record budget and a large stimulus package while proposing tax cuts. Japan's public debt exceeds 230 percent of annual economic output, the highest ratio among major economies. The shift matters well beyond Tokyo because Japan is one of the world's largest creditors and a major holder of foreign bonds.

One Perspective

Those wary of the trend warn that the government's expansive fiscal stance is adding fuel at the wrong moment. Record spending combined with tax cuts, and without a clearly identified source of revenue, risks pushing borrowing costs above the 3 percent level the budget assumed for servicing the national debt. Should that happen, a larger share of public money would go simply to paying interest, and investors already questioning how much Japanese debt the market can absorb could demand still higher yields, tightening the squeeze on the country's finances.

Another Perspective

Supporters of continued stimulus argue that after decades of fighting deflation, Japan needs to lock in growth and rising wages, and that some increase in yields is the natural price of finally escaping near zero rates. They note that a large share of Japanese government debt is held domestically and by the central bank itself, which gives Tokyo more room to manage its obligations than the headline debt ratio suggests. In this view the rise in yields reflects a return to normal conditions rather than an approaching crisis.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts stress that the move carries global stakes. Higher yields at home can draw Japanese capital back from foreign markets, including United States Treasuries, which could nudge borrowing costs upward abroad and unwind the carry trades that have long relied on cheap yen. The Bank of Japan faces a delicate balance between curbing inflation and avoiding a disorderly sell off in its own bond market. Whether the transition proves smooth or turbulent will depend in large part on how far the Takaichi government pushes its fiscal agenda and how convincingly it can reassure investors about the path of the national debt.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What exactly is the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, and how does it differ from an alliance like NATO?

A: The SCO is a Eurasian political, economic, and security grouping founded in 2001, now spanning members from China and Russia to India, Pakistan, Iran, and several Central Asian states. Unlike NATO, it has no mutual defense clause, no integrated military command, and no standing joint forces. It works mainly by consensus on broad declarations and cooperation in areas such as counterterrorism, trade, and energy, which makes it more a forum for coordination and signaling than a binding military alliance.

Q: Why do government bond yields matter to people outside the financial markets?

A: A government bond yield is essentially the interest rate a country pays to borrow, and its effects ripple outward. Higher yields raise the cost of servicing public debt, which can crowd out other spending, and they tend to lift borrowing costs for mortgages, business loans, and other credit. When yields rise sharply in a large economy like Japan's, they can also move exchange rates and pull investment across borders, affecting markets far from where the change began.

Q: Why have several countries in Africa's Sahel region seen repeated military coups?

A: The Sahel has faced a combination of spreading jihadist insurgencies, weak state institutions, economic hardship, and public frustration with governments seen as ineffective or corrupt. Militaries have used that discontent to justify seizing power, and a coup in one country can embolden officers in a neighboring one. Several of the new military governments have expelled former Western partners and turned to Russia for security support, though the coups have generally not resolved the underlying insecurity that helped trigger them.
