COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Xi Jinping Arrives in Washington for His First State Visit in Eleven Years

Est. read time: 4 min · Northeast Asia · Diplomacy

On September 23 President Xi Jinping began a three day state visit to the United States, his first visit to the White House in eleven years. President Trump broke with protocol to greet Xi personally on his arrival near Washington, with a formal ceremony, a summit meeting, and a state dinner attended by American technology executives planned for the days that followed. The two governments are using the visit to steady a relationship strained by tariffs, export controls, competition over advanced technology, and disputes over Taiwan. Xi chose to travel to Washington rather than address the United Nations General Assembly in New York the same week, a signal of the weight Beijing places on managing ties with the United States directly.

One Perspective

Supporters of the summit argue that direct contact between the two leaders is the most effective way to keep competition from sliding into open conflict. They point to a fragile trade truce that has held in recent months and contend that face to face diplomacy can lock in tariff relief, ease restrictions on critical goods, and create channels to manage crises before they escalate. In this view the pageantry of a state visit carries its own value, signaling to markets and to third countries that Washington and Beijing intend to coexist rather than fully decouple.

Another Perspective

Critics counter that the visit hands Beijing prestige without extracting durable concessions, and that China is negotiating from a position of growing strength, particularly its dominance of the rare earth minerals and processing that Western industry depends on. They warn that Washington risks trading away leverage on technology and security for short term commercial gains, and they note that Beijing has publicly drawn firm red lines around Taiwan, its political system, and its right to develop, leaving little room to resolve the disputes that most divide the two powers. From this angle the summit manages symptoms while the underlying rivalry deepens.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the meeting as an exercise in managing competition rather than ending it. The economic relationship is deeply interdependent, with the United States reliant on Chinese supply chains and China on access to American markets and advanced chips, which gives both sides reasons to avoid a rupture even as they compete. Past leader level meetings have tended to produce pauses and partial deals rather than lasting settlements, and the structural contest over technology, military power in the Pacific, and the future of Taiwan remains. The test of the visit will be whether it yields concrete and durable agreements or, like earlier summits, a temporary calm before the next round of friction.

SECURITY · HORN OF AFRICA

Seven Ethiopian Armed Groups Unite in a Bid to Topple Abiy Ahmed

Est. read time: 4 min · Horn of Africa · Conflict

In late September seven armed groups from across Ethiopia announced a new coalition, the Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival, with the declared aim of removing the government of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. The alliance brings together former enemies, including the Tigray People's Liberation Front and the Amhara Fano movement, which fought on opposite sides of the 2020 to 2022 civil war, alongside the Oromo Liberation Army and four smaller regional groups. The announcement comes as fighting continues in the Amhara region and tensions rise again in Tigray, raising fears of a return to large scale war in Africa's second most populous country. Its significance lies in the breadth of the coalition, which for the first time links insurgents from several of Ethiopia's major regions against a common target.

One Perspective

The groups behind the alliance present themselves as a broad front against a government they describe as authoritarian and exclusionary. They argue that Abiy has centralized power, marginalized their communities, and pursued military solutions to political grievances, pointing to the war in Amhara and the unresolved aftermath of the Tigray conflict. From this position the coalition is a survival pact among peoples who feel threatened, and its stated goal is to replace the current government with an inclusive transitional arrangement that they say would give Ethiopia's regions a genuine voice.

Another Perspective

Abiy's government and other observers question both the coalition's motives and its durability. They note that its members hold sharply different and at times opposing agendas, with Tigrayan and Amhara forces having recently fought each other over territory, and they argue that shared hostility to Abiy is a thin foundation for a lasting alliance. The government casts the armed groups as spoilers pursuing power by force rather than the ballot, and it points to economic growth and reforms as evidence that its approach is delivering. In this framing the alliance is a fragile marriage of convenience that could fracture over the same divisions it has set aside.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts caution that coalitions of this kind have often proved unstable, held together by a common enemy but strained by competing ambitions once that enemy is confronted. Ethiopia's fault lines run deep, and the 2020 to 2022 war killed a large number of people and displaced millions before a fragile peace deal was reached in Pretoria. A renewed and wider conflict would carry consequences well beyond Ethiopia's borders, threatening stability across the Horn of Africa, straining relations with neighbors, and disrupting a region that sits astride vital Red Sea trade routes. Whether the alliance becomes a serious military challenge or dissolves under its own contradictions will depend on how the government responds and whether outside actors are drawn in.

ECONOMICS · TRADE

The European Union and the Philippines Reach a Preliminary Trade Deal

Est. read time: 3 min · Europe and Southeast Asia · Trade

On September 22 the European Union and the Philippines announced that they had reached a preliminary agreement on a free trade deal, a step that officials said still requires further negotiation before a final text can be signed. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen described the understanding as a milestone, while the two sides framed it as a way to expand the roughly thirty billion euros in annual trade that already flows between the twenty seven nation bloc and the Southeast Asian country of about one hundred fifteen million people. The Philippines would become the third member of the ASEAN group, after Vietnam and Singapore, to strike a bilateral trade agreement with the European Union. The deal matters as another sign that both economies are seeking new partners at a time of rising tariffs and trade friction among the largest powers.

One Perspective

Backers of the agreement argue that it offers both sides a hedge against a more protectionist and unpredictable global economy. For the European Union, closer ties in Southeast Asia advance a strategy of diversifying supply chains and reducing dependence on any single large market, while for the Philippines the prospect of lower barriers to a wealthy consumer bloc promises investment, jobs, and access for its electronics and manufactured goods. Supporters say the timing is deliberate, a move to deepen reliable partnerships as tariffs imposed by larger economies disrupt established trade flows.

Another Perspective

Others urge caution, noting that the announcement is preliminary and that European trade agreements must clear a long process of legal review and ratification, where labor standards, environmental rules, and human rights concerns can slow or reshape a deal. Some domestic producers on both sides worry about competition from cheaper imports, and critics argue that the headline figures obscure how modest the immediate effects may be, since the details that determine who benefits are still unsettled. In this reading the agreement is a statement of intent whose real value will depend on negotiations yet to come.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts place the deal within a broader European push into Southeast Asia and a wider reordering of global trade. The bloc has moved to conclude agreements across the region as it seeks alternatives to reliance on China and looks to insulate itself from tariff disputes with the United States, and Southeast Asian governments have likewise sought to spread their trade relationships more widely. Such agreements typically take years to finalize and implement, and their economic impact tends to build gradually rather than arriving all at once. The Philippines deal is best understood as one piece of a slow realignment, in which middle sized economies court multiple partners to avoid being caught between competing giants.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What exactly is a state visit, and how is it different from other diplomatic meetings?

A: A state visit is the highest and most formal category of visit between countries, extended by one head of state to another and marked by ceremonial elements such as an official welcome, a guard of honor, and a state dinner. It differs from working visits or ordinary summit meetings, which center on negotiation with far less protocol, because much of its purpose is symbolic, signaling the importance each government attaches to the relationship. The formality does not guarantee substantive agreements, but it communicates intent to domestic and international audiences alike.

Q: Why do alliances between different armed or political groups so often struggle to last?

A: Coalitions formed to oppose a common adversary can unite groups whose longer term goals diverge or even conflict. While a shared threat provides a reason to cooperate, questions of leadership, territory, and what should replace the existing order frequently resurface once the immediate danger recedes or the balance of power shifts. History offers many examples of wartime alliances that fractured soon after their common purpose was achieved, which is why analysts watch not only whether such groups can fight together but whether they can agree on what comes next.

Q: What is a free trade agreement, and why do these deals take so long to finalize?

A: A free trade agreement is a treaty in which countries agree to lower or remove tariffs and other barriers to commerce between them, and often to align rules on standards, investment, and dispute resolution. They take time because they touch many sensitive areas, from agriculture and manufacturing to labor and the environment, and because each provision creates winners and losers whose interests must be balanced. In large blocs such as the European Union, a deal must also pass legal review and be ratified by multiple governments and legislatures, a process that can stretch a preliminary understanding into years of further work.
