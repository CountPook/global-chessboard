COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Washington Invites Putin to a G20 Summit on American Soil

Est. read time: 4 min · US-Russia · Diplomacy

On September 23, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the Trump administration had invited Russian President Vladimir Putin to the Group of 20 summit the United States will host in December at the Trump National Doral resort near Miami. It would be Putin's first visit to the continental United States in years, and it comes as Russia's war in Ukraine grinds on. The Kremlin said it was grateful for the invitation but had made no decision, while Rubio framed it as a chance for Putin to engage with President Trump and other leaders. The move matters because it would begin to break the diplomatic isolation that Western governments have tried to impose on Moscow since the 2022 invasion.

One Perspective

Supporters of the invitation argue that ending the war in Ukraine will require direct dialogue with the leader who can order Russian forces to stop, and that isolating Moscow has not changed its conduct on the battlefield. From this view, hosting Putin alongside other heads of state creates leverage and keeps a fragile diplomatic track alive, building on the Trump and Putin meeting in Anchorage in 2025. Rubio has said Washington remains open to engaging Russia on Ukraine, and proponents see the summit as a venue to test whether Moscow is serious about a settlement.

Another Perspective

Critics counter that welcoming Putin to American soil rewards aggression and undercuts Ukraine and the European allies who have sanctioned Russia and armed Kyiv. They note that the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin in 2023 over the alleged unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children, and they argue that a summit invitation helps normalize a leader many Western governments treat as a pariah. For these observers, the gesture risks fracturing allied unity in exchange for a meeting that may produce no concrete result.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the United States, as G20 host, holds wide discretion over invitations, and that Washington is not a member of the International Criminal Court and would not be obliged to act on its warrant. Whether Putin actually attends is a separate question, and the Kremlin's noncommittal reply leaves room to negotiate terms through diplomatic channels. History offers mixed lessons, as high-profile summits have occasionally produced breakthroughs but more often reflect rather than resolve the disputes behind them. The practical test will be whether the invitation advances a Ukraine settlement or simply stages a meeting without substance.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Washington Post, Axios.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

Trump and Iran Trade Threats and Overtures at the United Nations

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Nuclear Diplomacy

The annual gathering of world leaders in New York became a stage for the standoff between Washington and Tehran. In his address, President Trump said he was weighing whether to reach a deal with Iran or to annihilate the Islamic Republic, and he repeated that he would not allow it to build a nuclear weapon. A day earlier, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian struck a defiant tone, saying Iran would not surrender, would never build a nuclear bomb, and would not give up its right to peaceful nuclear technology, while blaming the United States and Israel for regional instability. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Iran's foreign minister on the sidelines in a session mediated by Qatar, though Rubio cautioned against describing it as a breakthrough.

One Perspective

Washington and its ally Israel argue that only sustained pressure, backed by a credible threat of force, can stop Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon. In this framing, Iran's enrichment program and its support for armed groups across the region make a strong deterrent essential, and earlier diplomacy failed to permanently close Iran's path to a bomb. Supporters of this approach say the offer of a deal remains on the table, but that Tehran must accept firm limits and intrusive inspections before sanctions ease.

Another Perspective

Tehran maintains that its nuclear program is peaceful and lawful under the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and that it has a sovereign right to enrich uranium for civilian purposes. Iranian officials point to strikes on their territory and personnel as evidence that the United States and Israel, not Iran, are driving escalation, and they argue that threats of annihilation violate the UN Charter. From this position, Iran will negotiate only as an equal and will not dismantle capabilities it regards as a national right.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that diplomacy between Washington and Tehran has repeatedly stalled since the collapse of the 2015 nuclear agreement, and that military strikes during the recent cycle of confrontation damaged facilities without ending the program. The International Atomic Energy Agency has warned that monitoring gaps make it harder to track Iran's enriched uranium, which raises the stakes of any diplomatic failure. A negotiated ceiling on enrichment paired with inspections is the outcome most nonproliferation experts favor, yet deep mistrust on both sides and domestic politics in each capital make it hard to reach. The alternative, a wider war, would carry heavy costs across the region and global energy markets.

GLOBAL SOUTH · CENTRAL AFRICA

Congo and Rwanda Test a Fragile Peace as Fighting Persists in the East

Est. read time: 4 min · Central Africa · Conflict

Negotiators returned to Geneva in mid-September to try to hold together a set of overlapping peace efforts aimed at ending the conflict in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where the Rwanda-backed M23 group seized the cities of Goma and Bukavu in early 2025. The talks build on a US-brokered agreement between Kinshasa and Kigali and a separate Qatar-mediated framework between the Congolese government and M23, and in August the two sides agreed a roadmap for further negotiations. Even so, fighting has continued in North Kivu while diplomats meet, and the war has displaced an estimated 5.3 million people. The outcome will shape stability across Africa's Great Lakes region.

One Perspective

The Congolese government argues that the root of the conflict is external, contending that neighboring Rwanda arms and directs M23 to seize territory and mineral wealth, a charge Kigali denies. From Kinshasa's position, a durable peace requires the withdrawal of foreign-backed fighters, the restoration of Congolese state authority over occupied areas, and the safe return of displaced civilians. Congolese officials say they will keep negotiating but will not accept an outcome that ratifies their territorial losses.

Another Perspective

Rwanda and M23 frame the fighting as a response to insecurity and to the presence of the FDLR, an armed group with roots in the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi, which Kigali says operates alongside Congolese forces. M23 presents itself as defending Congolese Tutsi communities it says the government has marginalized, and Rwanda argues that its security concerns must be addressed for any settlement to hold. In this view, a lasting deal has to tackle governance grievances inside Congo, not only the movement of troops across the border.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that eastern Congo's mineral wealth, including deposits critical to global technology supply chains, has long fueled and financed the fighting, giving outside actors a stake in the outcome. Previous ceasefires and frameworks in the region have repeatedly broken down, and the current process still leaves several difficult protocols unresolved, from the return of refugees to accountability for atrocities. International monitors and regional bodies have pressed both sides to implement what they have signed, but implementation, rather than the signing of documents, has always been the harder task. Whether this round proves different will depend on verification on the ground and sustained pressure from the powers backing each party.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Africanews, Security Council Report.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What is the G20, and why does hosting it give a country influence?

A: The Group of 20 is a forum of major economies, spanning both wealthy industrial states and large emerging markets, that together account for most of the world's economic output. The country holding the rotating presidency sets much of the agenda and hosts the leaders' summit, which gives it a platform to shape priorities and to decide, within limits, who is invited. Hosting also creates room for side meetings, where a great deal of the real diplomacy at such gatherings takes place.

Q: What is an International Criminal Court arrest warrant, and who is bound by it?

A: The International Criminal Court is a treaty-based tribunal that prosecutes individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity when national courts cannot or will not. Its arrest warrants bind only the roughly 120 states that are party to its founding statute, which are expected to detain a wanted person who enters their territory. Countries outside the treaty, including the United States, Russia, and China, are not obligated to act on its warrants, which limits the court's practical reach.

Q: Why does uranium enrichment sit at the center of nuclear negotiations?

A: Enrichment raises the proportion of a particular uranium isotope, and the same technology can produce fuel for civilian power reactors at low levels or material for a weapon at much higher levels. Because a state that masters enrichment holds the key step toward a bomb, negotiators focus on capping how much a country can enrich, to what level, and under what monitoring. That is why inspections and limits on enrichment, rather than the existence of a nuclear program itself, tend to dominate the talks.
