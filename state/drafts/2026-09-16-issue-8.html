The BRICS Bloc Expands Its Ambitions in New Delhi

Est. read time: 4 min · Multilateralism · Global South

The eighteenth BRICS summit met at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi on September 12 and 13, 2026, the first hosted by India since the bloc grew to eleven members. Leaders adopted the New Delhi Declaration, a text running to roughly 140 paragraphs that spans trade, technology, and global governance. Russian President Vladimir Putin attended in person, his first BRICS summit held abroad since Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, and held talks with Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The declaration criticized unilateral sanctions and tariffs imposed without United Nations Security Council authorization, and it pressed for reform of the Security Council and the International Monetary Fund, greater use of local currencies in trade, and common rules for artificial intelligence.

One Perspective

Supporters cast BRICS as an engine of a more multipolar order. In this view the bloc gives the Global South a larger voice, challenges institutions long dominated by the West, and reduces dependence on the United States dollar in cross border settlement. India framed its chairmanship, under the theme of building for resilience, innovation, cooperation, and sustainability, as constructive rather than confrontational. The declaration's language on sanctions reflects a shared grievance among members that have faced tariffs and penalties from Washington, among them China, Russia, and India.

Another Perspective

Skeptics, among them many Western officials and analysts, argue that BRICS remains a loose grouping of states with divergent interests and limited capacity for binding action. They point to the declaration's careful silence on Ukraine, which offered only a generic appeal for dialogue without naming Russia, as evidence of internal strain. India, Brazil, and others maintain close ties with the United States and Europe, while Russia and China favor a more openly anti Western posture. Critics also contend that talk of moving away from the dollar runs well ahead of what members are able or willing to deliver.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the enlarged bloc now accounts for roughly half of the world's population and a substantial share of global output and energy production, which lends weight to its statements even without enforcement tools. History suggests that as such groupings widen their reach, they often trade away cohesion, and the presence of rivals such as Saudi Arabia and Iran, or Egypt and Ethiopia, makes consensus harder to reach. The summit's practical significance may rest less in the text of the declaration than in its optics, from Putin's return to a major multilateral stage to India's effort to position itself as a bridge between competing blocs ahead of China's turn in the chair in 2027.

EUROPE · SWEDEN

Sweden's Far Right Stumbles in a Photo Finish Election

Est. read time: 3 min · Elections · European Union

Swedish voters went to the polls on September 13, 2026 to elect the 349 members of the Riksdag in an unusually close contest. Early counts indicated that the anti immigration Sweden Democrats had slipped to around 17.5 percent, down about three points from the 20.5 percent they won in 2022, while the Social Democrats led with roughly 28 percent. With overseas ballots and a number of districts still to be counted, party leaders declined to claim victory or concede, leaving the balance between the left and right blocs unresolved.

One Perspective

The center left opposition and its allies read the early results as a rejection of the outgoing center right government, which had governed with support from the Sweden Democrats. In this reading, voters drew back from the prospect of a nationalist party entering the cabinet for the first time, and the decline in the Sweden Democrats' share suggests to them that hardline migration politics has reached a ceiling.

Another Perspective

The governing bloc and the Sweden Democrats counter that the arithmetic between the two sides remains tight and that public concern over gang violence, crime, and immigration has not faded. Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson had signaled a willingness to offer the Sweden Democrats cabinet posts if his bloc held its majority, and his camp argues that the outcome cannot be settled until the final tally, including votes cast abroad, is complete.

The Strategic Reality

Sweden's proportional system routinely produces narrow blocs and lengthy coalition negotiations, and the government formed in 2022 took weeks to assemble. Whatever the final count shows, the sustained strength of the Sweden Democrats has already pulled the mainstream toward tougher policies on migration and security, a pattern visible elsewhere in Europe, including recent gains for the Alternative for Germany. The result will shape Stockholm's stance on defense spending within NATO and on migration rules inside the European Union at a tense moment for the continent.

ECONOMICS · MONETARY POLICY

The Federal Reserve Turns Toward Its First Rate Hike Since 2023

Est. read time: 3 min · Monetary Policy · Global Markets

As the United States Federal Reserve concluded a two day meeting on September 16, 2026, futures markets priced in a roughly 93 percent chance that policymakers would raise the benchmark interest rate by a quarter point, to a range of 3.75 to 4.00 percent. Such a move would mark the first increase since 2023 and would lift rates from the 3.50 to 3.75 percent range held since December 2025. The decision falls to Chair Kevin Warsh, who was sworn in during May 2026, and it comes with inflation stuck above target, at 3.4 percent for headline consumer prices in August and 2.4 percent at the core, pushed higher in part by energy costs linked to conflict in the Middle East.

One Perspective

Those who favor tightening argue that price stability requires a firmer hand. With inflation above the 2 percent target for years and energy prices climbing, they contend that the central bank must act to keep long term inflation expectations from drifting, even at the cost of slower growth. In this view, a Fed seen as tolerating persistent inflation risks its credibility, and restoring that credibility is worth a modest increase in borrowing costs.

Another Perspective

Others warn that raising rates into a softening labor market and a supply driven price shock could push the economy toward recession. Because much of the current inflation stems from energy costs set by geopolitics rather than from excess demand, they argue that interest rate increases are a blunt instrument that cannot bring those prices down. Critics also note the perennial tension between the Fed's independence and political pressure for cheaper credit, which any tightening cycle tends to sharpen.

The Strategic Reality

Economists describe a familiar dilemma for central banks confronting supply driven inflation. Higher rates cool demand but cannot lower the energy prices that geopolitics dictates, so the Fed risks slowing growth without fully taming inflation. The decision also carries weight beyond American borders. A higher United States rate tends to strengthen the dollar, which pressures emerging market currencies and debt, a live concern for many of the same Global South economies that gathered at the BRICS summit. A first increase in three years signals that, for now, the Fed judges the risk of entrenched inflation to be greater than the risk of recession.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What does de-dollarization mean, and can BRICS actually achieve it?

A: De-dollarization refers to reducing reliance on the United States dollar for trade, reserves, and cross border payments. BRICS members have discussed settling more trade in their own currencies and building alternative payment channels. In practice the dollar still dominates global reserves and invoicing, and members disagree on how far to go, so most analysts expect gradual diversification rather than any rapid displacement.

Q: Why do central banks raise interest rates when inflation is driven by war and energy prices?

A: Rate increases work mainly by cooling demand and by keeping long term inflation expectations anchored. When inflation comes from supply shocks such as war driven energy costs, higher rates cannot lower those prices directly, so policymakers weigh the danger of expectations becoming entrenched against the danger of slowing an already strained economy. It is a matter of judgment rather than precise calculation.

Q: Why does a far right party's vote share in one small country matter beyond its borders?

A: A member state's government helps shape shared positions across the European Union and, in Sweden's case, within NATO as well, on questions from migration to defense spending to policy toward Russia. Shifts toward or away from nationalist parties in one country also serve as a signal of wider continental trends and affect how the bloc negotiates common rules.
