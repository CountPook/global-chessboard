COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Washington Bars the Palestinian Delegation as the UN Votes to Let Abbas Speak by Video

Est. read time: 4 min · United Nations · Diplomacy

On September 16 the United States State Department said it would extend visa restrictions on officials of the Palestinian Authority and the Palestine Liberation Organization, barring President Mahmoud Abbas from traveling to New York to address the United Nations General Assembly for the second year in a row. Two days later the General Assembly voted 152 to 3, with 4 abstentions, to allow Abbas to deliver his speech through a prerecorded video, with only the United States, Israel, and Paraguay opposed. The dispute arrived just before the assembly's general debate was due to open on September 22, and it turned a procedural fight over visas into a wider contest over recognition of Palestinian statehood and the reach of American power over access to the world body.

One Perspective

Washington and Israel argue that the Palestinian Authority has not earned a platform at the United Nations. They point to what they describe as a failure to reform its institutions, to curb payments tied to militancy, and to abandon a strategy of pursuing statehood through international bodies rather than direct talks with Israel. From this position, granting visas would reward the internationalizing of the conflict, and the United States, as the host of the United Nations, contends that it may withhold entry on security and policy grounds. Israeli officials frame the recognition drive as a symbolic move that sidesteps the negotiations they say are the only route to a durable settlement.

Another Perspective

The Palestinian leadership, the European Union, and the large majority that backed the video address argue that denying entry to a delegation with legitimate United Nations business undermines the body's universal character and the host country's obligations. They note that a wave of recognitions over the past year by states including the United Kingdom, France, and Canada has shifted the diplomatic ground, and that silencing Abbas in the assembly hall looks like an effort to slow that momentum. In this view the near unanimous vote is a rebuke to Washington and a defense of the principle that no single state should decide who may speak at the United Nations.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the quarrel revives a long running tension in the 1947 agreement that seats the United Nations in New York, which obliges the United States to admit those coming on United Nations business while leaving Washington room to claim national security exceptions. Similar disputes have surfaced before, and the assembly has previously voted to let barred leaders appear by video, so the workaround is not new. The deeper question is whether the recent recognitions translate into leverage on the ground, where the Palestinian Authority governs only part of the West Bank, Gaza lies in ruins, and negotiations remain frozen. The vote left the United States and Israel isolated on the procedural issue, but it did not change the underlying balance of power that keeps a two state outcome distant.

ECONOMICS · MONETARY POLICY

The Federal Reserve Raises Rates for the First Time Since 2023

Est. read time: 3 min · United States · Monetary Policy

On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter of a percentage point, lifting the target range to between 3.75 and 4 percent, its first increase in more than three years. The decision, approved by a unanimous vote of the rate setting committee, reversed the direction of policy after a long stretch of holding and cutting, and officials signaled in their projections that one more increase is likely before the end of 2026. The move matters well beyond the United States, because the dollar sits at the center of global finance and shifts in American rates ripple through currencies, borrowing costs, and capital flows around the world.

One Perspective

Supporters of the increase argue that the central bank had little choice once inflation began climbing again, driven in large part by a surge in oil and energy prices. In this view, allowing price rises to become embedded would be far more damaging than acting early, and a credible central bank must defend its target even when the shock originates outside its control. Raising rates now, the argument runs, anchors expectations, protects the bank's credibility, and lowers the risk of a harsher correction later.

Another Perspective

Critics counter that tightening into an energy driven price shock risks punishing households and businesses for costs that higher rates cannot lower. Monetary policy does not produce more oil, they note, and squeezing demand to offset a supply shock can slow growth and weaken the labor market without addressing the root cause. Some also point to the political backdrop, warning that a central bank raising borrowing costs into a fragile economy invites pressure and second guessing from elected officials.

The Strategic Reality

Economists describe supply driven inflation as one of the hardest problems a central bank can face, echoing the dilemmas of the 1970s, when energy shocks forced policymakers to choose between growth and price stability. The Federal Reserve carries a dual mandate to seek both stable prices and maximum employment, and an oil price spike pushes those goals in opposite directions. Higher American rates also tend to strengthen the dollar, which can ease inflation at home while raising the cost of dollar debt for developing economies abroad. The signaled path of one further increase suggests the bank intends to hold a firm line, while leaving itself room to pause if the energy shock fades.

EUROPE · ELECTIONS

Sweden's Center-Left Wins a Narrow Majority and Andersson Prepares to Return

Est. read time: 3 min · Sweden · Elections

A bloc of center left parties led by the Social Democrats won Sweden's general election held on September 13, taking 176 of the 349 seats in the Riksdag against 173 for the governing right wing coalition, a majority of just three seats once the count was completed in the days that followed. The result unseats Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson and puts Magdalena Andersson, who lost power in 2022, in line to return as prime minister. The vote also marked the first parliamentary setback for the Sweden Democrats, the anti immigration party that had propped up the government, whose support slipped to roughly 17.6 percent, about three points below its 2022 showing.

One Perspective

For the incoming bloc, the outcome reads as a verdict on a government that leaned on the Sweden Democrats and struggled with the cost of living and public services. Supporters argue that voters pulled back from the hard right and chose a return to the pragmatic, welfare focused governance the Social Democrats have long represented. They see the far right party's decline as evidence that its rise was not inevitable and that mainstream parties can still set the terms of Swedish politics.

Another Perspective

The outgoing coalition and its allies stress how narrow the margin is and question whether a three seat majority amounts to a mandate. They point out that the Sweden Democrats remain one of the largest parties and that the right governed through a period of tough measures on gang crime and steered the country into NATO. In this framing the left's coalition is fragile and divided, and the government that emerges may struggle to hold together long enough to deliver on its promises.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that a majority this thin places enormous weight on coalition arithmetic, and Andersson must now bridge sharp differences among the Social Democrats, the Greens, the Left Party, and the Centre Party, some of which disagree over whether the Left should sit in cabinet. Sweden's consensus driven system has produced drawn out government formations before, and talks could stretch on for weeks. On foreign and security policy, continuity is likely, since support for NATO membership and for Ukraine spans most of the major parties. The larger signal may be the plateau of the Sweden Democrats, a data point that will be studied closely by those tracking the trajectory of the European hard right.

Sources: France 24, NBC News, ABC News

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why does it matter whether a leader speaks at the United Nations in person or by video?

A: In person attendance at the General Assembly is about far more than the speech itself. The corridors and side rooms of the annual gathering host a dense schedule of private meetings, where deals are shaped and disputes are managed away from the podium. A leader confined to a video link can still deliver a message, but loses the informal diplomacy that often matters most, and the denial of entry itself becomes a political signal about the host country's stance.

Q: Can a central bank actually bring down inflation that is caused by high oil prices?

A: Only indirectly, and at a cost. Interest rates work by cooling demand, borrowing, and spending, which can ease price pressures that come from an overheating economy. When inflation is driven by a supply shock such as a jump in oil prices, higher rates cannot add barrels to the market, so the bank can only lower inflation by slowing the wider economy. That is why energy driven inflation forces uncomfortable trade offs between stable prices and growth.

Q: Why do countries with proportional representation so often end up with coalition governments?

A: Proportional systems award seats in rough proportion to each party's share of the vote, which tends to produce parliaments with several sizable parties rather than two dominant ones. Because a single party rarely wins an outright majority, forming a government usually requires bargaining among several parties to reach a combined majority. This can produce broad and representative coalitions, but also fragile ones, since the withdrawal of a single partner can bring a government down.
