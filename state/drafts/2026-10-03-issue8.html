COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Ethiopia and Eritrea Sever Ties as Drones Strike Addis Ababa

Est. read time: 4 min · Horn of Africa · Conflict

On October 1 Ethiopia shut its embassy in the Eritrean capital Asmara and declared ten Eritrean diplomats unwelcome, accusing Asmara of arming and sheltering rebels in the northern Tigray region. Eritrea answered within a day by cutting its own diplomatic ties with Addis Ababa. The rupture came only hours after drones struck near the headquarters of Ethiopia's military and other sites in the capital, an attack claimed by a figure linked to the Tigray People's Liberation Front, which had resumed fighting the federal government days earlier for the first time since a 2022 ceasefire. It is the sharpest confrontation between the two neighbors since they signed a peace agreement in 2018 that ended two decades of hostility, and it revives the prospect of open conflict in one of the world's most volatile regions.

One Perspective

Ethiopia's government frames the break as a response to foreign subversion. Officials in Addis Ababa say Eritrea has been supplying and encouraging Tigrayan fighters in order to destabilize the federal state, and they cast the expulsion of Eritrean diplomats as a defensive step against interference in Ethiopia's internal affairs. From this vantage point the drone strikes on the capital are evidence of a coordinated effort to reopen a war the 2022 ceasefire was meant to close, and the government presents its measures as necessary to protect national security and sovereignty.

Another Perspective

Eritrea rejects the accusation and portrays Ethiopia as the aggressor. Asmara has previously denied arming Tigrayan forces and has accused Ethiopian troops of operating near its border, while warning against what it sees as Addis Ababa's ambition to secure access to the Red Sea coast, much of which lies along Eritrean territory. Tigrayan factions, for their part, argue that the promises of the 2022 settlement, including the withdrawal of outside forces and the restoration of basic services, were never fully honored, leaving grievances that have now boiled over. In this telling the crisis grows out of unfinished business from the last war rather than fresh Eritrean aggression.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts warn that the region is sliding back toward a confrontation it can ill afford. The 1998 to 2000 border war between Ethiopia and Eritrea killed tens of thousands before ending in a stalemate, and the 2018 rapprochement that earned Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed a Nobel Peace Prize now looks increasingly fragile. Observers point to Ethiopia's landlocked status and its stated desire for a seaport as a structural source of friction with its coastal neighbors, and to the unresolved status of Tigray as a persistent trigger. With Sudan's war still raging to the west, a fresh rupture in the Horn risks drawing in armed groups and outside powers across an already unstable belt of the continent.

ENERGY · GLOBAL SHIPPING

The Strait of Hormuz Stays Shut as Reopening Talks Stall

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Energy

More than seven months after Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz to foreign shipping, the world's most important oil chokepoint remains effectively shut, and efforts to reopen it are deadlocked. Iran sealed the strait at the end of February in response to a United States and Israeli air campaign against its territory, choking off a waterway that in normal times carries a large share of the world's seaborne crude. Indirect talks between Washington and Tehran resumed in late September through Qatari mediators, but the two sides remain far apart. France, meanwhile, has begun circulating a draft United Nations Security Council resolution calling for freedom of navigation to be restored. The standoff matters because a prolonged closure strains global energy supplies and keeps a major power confrontation simmering at one of the planet's critical crossroads.

One Perspective

Tehran casts the closure as a legitimate act of self defense and a source of leverage. Iranian officials argue that they shut the strait only after being attacked, and that it will stay closed until the United States lifts its naval blockade and eases sanctions. Iran has floated a phased roadmap under which the blockade and sanctions would be removed first, the strait would reopen about a week later, and negotiations over its nuclear program would follow. In this framing the waterway is one of the few cards Tehran holds, and it will not surrender that card without firm guarantees.

Another Perspective

Washington and many Gulf states see the closure as unlawful coercion that cannot be rewarded. The United States has rejected Iran's sequencing, insisting that a chokepoint used by the whole world should not be held hostage to one country's demands, and President Trump turned down the seven day plan in late September. Gulf exporters, whose economies depend on the strait, want it reopened without preconditions, and France has grounded its draft resolution in the international law of the sea, arguing that safe passage is a global public good rather than a bilateral bargaining chip. From this side, conceding on Iran's terms would invite future pressure at other maritime chokepoints.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that a full, sustained closure of the strait had long been treated as a worst case scenario, and that its persistence is testing assumptions about global energy resilience. Shipping through the waterway has fallen to a trickle from its usual volume, and while buyers have drawn on stockpiles, alternative pipelines, and other suppliers, no route fully replaces it. An earlier Security Council effort to demand the strait be reopened was blocked this year by Russian and Chinese vetoes, which leaves France's new initiative facing an uncertain path. History suggests such crises eventually ease through negotiation, but the longer the strait stays shut, the greater the risk of miscalculation among the warships now crowded into a narrow sea.

DIPLOMACY · EAST ASIA

Seoul Threatens Action Against Kyiv Over a Prisoner Transfer

Est. read time: 3 min · East Asia · Diplomacy

A public quarrel has broken out between South Korea and Ukraine, two partners of the West, over the handling of two North Korean soldiers captured while fighting for Russia. During the United Nations General Assembly, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Kyiv had transferred the two prisoners to South Korea after they were taken on the battlefield in Russia's Kursk region. Seoul reacted with anger, as President Lee Jae Myung accused Ukraine of breaching an agreement to keep the transfer confidential and demanded an apology. On October 2 Lee warned that his government would take unspecified additional measures if Kyiv continued to deny that such an understanding existed. The episode is a rare open rift between two governments that both align closely with the United States and Europe.

One Perspective

Seoul says Ukraine broke a promise and endangered the men. South Korean officials maintain that the transfer was meant to be kept quiet in order to protect the two soldiers, who are North Korean nationals, and that publicizing it exposed them and complicated a sensitive arrangement. Lee has said he tried to handle the matter diplomatically but was made to look dishonest when Kyiv denied that any confidentiality deal had been struck. He has also objected to Ukrainian comments suggesting a looming clash between North and South Korea, calling them reckless talk that risks inflaming the peninsula.

Another Perspective

Ukraine denies that it agreed to secrecy and sees the episode differently. Kyiv has an interest in publicizing the presence of North Korean troops fighting alongside Russian forces, which it uses to underline the international dimension of the war and to press for more Western support. Ukrainian officials have disputed Seoul's account of a confidentiality agreement, and from their perspective, disclosing that captured North Koreans were sent to South Korea is a legitimate part of making the case that Pyongyang is an active participant in the conflict. The clash shows how the war in Europe increasingly entangles the security politics of East Asia.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts see the spat as a symptom of a deeper convergence between the European and Asian theaters. North Korea's dispatch of troops and munitions to support Russia has blurred the line between the two regions, drawing South Korea, long focused on its own peninsula, into the diplomacy of the Ukraine war. The handling of defectors and prisoners is legally and politically delicate, since the men are claimed as citizens by a state they were sent to fight for, and their fate touches on the Geneva Conventions as well as the rivalry between the two Koreas. For now the rift is diplomatic rather than strategic, but it shows how a single battlefield decision can ripple across alliances on opposite sides of the world.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Korea Herald, Taipei Times

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What makes the Strait of Hormuz so important to the global economy?

A: It is the only sea route from the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, and in normal times a large share of the world's seaborne oil and a significant volume of liquefied natural gas pass through it. Because the channel is narrow and there is no pipeline network able to fully bypass it, even the threat of a disruption can move energy prices worldwide. That combination of high volume and few alternatives is what gives the waterway its outsized strategic weight.

Q: Why do neighboring states that once made peace, such as Ethiopia and Eritrea, slide back toward confrontation?

A: Peace agreements often freeze a conflict without resolving the grievances beneath it. Border demarcation, the status of contested regions, access to trade routes or the sea, and the ambitions of leaders on each side can all remain live after the fighting stops. When one government believes the other is backing internal rebels, or when a settlement's promises go unfulfilled, old suspicions reassert themselves quickly, and a single incident can tip a fragile calm back toward open hostility.

Q: What legal status do foreign soldiers captured in another country's war have?

A: Under the Geneva Conventions, members of an armed force who are captured are generally entitled to prisoner of war protections regardless of their nationality. Complications arise when a captured soldier is a national of a third country, or wishes to defect rather than be repatriated, since the detaining power must weigh its legal obligations against the individual's safety and the political sensitivities involved. Such cases are usually handled one by one and negotiated quietly between the governments concerned.
