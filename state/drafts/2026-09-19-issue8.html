COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Russia's Wartime Duma Vote Unfolds With the Opposition Locked Out

Est. read time: 4 min · Russia · Elections

Russians began three days of voting on September 18 to fill the 450 seats of the State Duma, the lower house of parliament, in the first such election since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. President Vladimir Putin, who cast his ballot online, has framed the vote as a measure of public support for the war, while the Kremlin-aligned United Russia party is widely expected to keep its commanding majority. With most anti-war and independent candidates barred and independent monitors absent, the contest is less about the result than about the message the Kremlin intends it to send.

One Perspective

To the Kremlin and United Russia, the election is a demonstration of national unity and continuity at a moment of confrontation with the West. Officials present the three-day format, expanded online voting, and turnout drives as evidence of a modern and accessible system, and they cast the expected outcome as a mandate to continue the war and resist outside pressure. In this account, the exclusion of certain parties and candidates reflects lawful enforcement of rules on foreign funding and extremism rather than political engineering.

Another Perspective

Critics, including exiled opposition figures and Western governments, describe the vote as engineered before polls even opened. They point to the barring of the pro-peace Yabloko party from the party-list ballot, the labeling of war critics as foreign agents, the imprisonment or flight of opposition leaders, and the removal of independent observers as evidence the outcome was preordained. One European diplomat called the elections "pretty much a sham," and analysts argue that a multi-day, largely electronic process makes the results almost impossible to verify.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts who track Russian politics note that managed elections serve a specific function for the Kremlin, converting administrative control into a ritual of legitimacy rather than a genuine contest for power. Voting is also being organized in occupied regions of Ukraine, which Kyiv and most of the international community reject as illegitimate. Whatever the tallies, the more telling signals will be the turnout figures the Kremlin chooses to publicize and whether the war-era political system shows any strain beneath a tightly controlled surface.

SECURITY · RED SEA

The Houthis Tighten Their Grip on the Bab al-Mandeb Strait

Est. read time: 4 min · Yemen · Maritime Security

Yemen's Houthi movement has seized control of the country's entire Red Sea coastline and pushed onto islands inside the Bab al-Mandeb strait, the narrow passage linking the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. The rapid advance, which took in the port of Mokha and the islands of Mayun, also known as Perim, and the Hanish group, hands the Iran-aligned movement its largest territorial gains in years and a dominant position over one of the world's busiest shipping lanes. It unfolds against the wider confrontation between Iran and the United States and its partners, with oil trading above 100 dollars a barrel.

One Perspective

The Houthis and their backers present the advance as both a battlefield victory over the internationally recognized government and a source of strategic leverage. By threatening a second maritime chokepoint alongside the contested Strait of Hormuz, the movement can pressure global energy markets, and by extension Washington, without directly confronting the US military. In talks with American officials in Muscat, Houthi representatives reportedly said they had no intention of attacking US shipping, a signal that the leverage is meant to be applied selectively.

Another Perspective

For Gulf states, shipping companies, and Western governments, the seizure threatens the free flow of trade and energy. Major carriers have kept diverting vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, adding weeks to voyages and driving up costs, while Saudi Arabia has faced repeated strikes on its shipping and oil infrastructure. Washington, however, has signaled it does not intend to open a new front, citing stretched military resources and domestic opposition to a widening war, a restraint that some regional partners see as leaving them exposed.

The Strategic Reality

Control of Bab al-Mandeb does not require sinking ships to matter, because the credible threat of disruption is enough to reshape insurance costs, routing, and prices. Historically, chokepoint coercion has been a tool of weaker actors seeking outsized influence, and analysts note the Houthis have absorbed years of strikes without losing capacity. The central question is whether the movement treats its new position as a bargaining chip to be traded in negotiations or as a lasting lever over global commerce.

ECONOMICS · MONETARY POLICY

The Federal Reserve Raises Rates as Oil Drives Inflation Higher

Est. read time: 3 min · United States · Central Banking

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter point on September 16 to a target range of 3.75 to 4 percent, its first increase since 2023. The unanimous decision came as inflation stayed well above the central bank's 2 percent goal, pushed higher by energy costs tied to the conflict in the Middle East. It also set the Fed on a collision course with the White House, which had publicly pressed for lower borrowing costs.

One Perspective

Supporters of the increase argue the Fed had little choice. With inflation running above target for an extended stretch and oil prices climbing, holding or cutting rates risked letting price expectations drift loose, a condition that is far harder to reverse once it sets in. In this view, the decision, and the chair's willingness to defy political pressure, reinforces the central bank's independence and its credibility as an inflation fighter, qualities that tend to lower long-term borrowing costs over time.

Another Perspective

The administration and some economists warn the move courts unnecessary economic pain. The president and senior officials argued that higher rates will slow growth and hiring at a delicate moment, and that inflation driven by an oil shock is a supply problem that rate increases cannot fix. Mark Zandi of Moody's cautioned that the odds of a serious Fed policy mistake are, in his words, uncomfortably high and rising, warning that tightening too aggressively could set off layoffs and a self-reinforcing downturn.

The Strategic Reality

The clash exposes a deeper tension in US economic policy, between a central bank built to act independently and a political system that bears the electoral cost of high rates and high prices. Supply-driven inflation, the kind an oil shock produces, is notoriously hard to tame with monetary tools alone, leaving the Fed to weigh credibility against growth. With markets pricing in the chance of another increase before year's end, the decision will ripple through global borrowing costs, currencies, and debt-heavy emerging economies well beyond American shores.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why do authoritarian governments bother holding elections they are sure to win?

A: Managed elections serve purposes beyond choosing leaders. They project an image of legitimacy at home and abroad, give officials a controlled way to gauge the public mood, reward loyal elites with office, and keep the machinery of the state in practice. The performance of consent can be as useful to a government as consent itself.

Q: What makes a maritime chokepoint like Bab al-Mandeb so important?

A: Chokepoints are narrow passages that funnel a large share of global trade into a small and easily disrupted space. Bab al-Mandeb channels traffic between Europe, the Gulf, and Asia, so a credible threat there forces ships onto far longer routes and raises costs across supply chains. Whoever can plausibly threaten to close such a passage gains leverage far out of proportion to their military size.

Q: Can a central bank actually control inflation caused by rising oil prices?

A: Only in part. Rate increases work by cooling demand, which can ease inflation that comes from an overheating economy. When prices rise because of a supply shock, such as a jump in oil costs, higher rates do little to add barrels to the market and instead risk slowing growth. Central banks often raise rates anyway, mainly to keep the public's expectations of future inflation from drifting upward.
