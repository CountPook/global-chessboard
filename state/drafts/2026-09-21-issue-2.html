Tokyo Weighs a 3.5 Percent Defense Spending Target

Est. read time: 4 min · Indo-Pacific · Defense

Japan's government is considering a medium-term goal of raising defense spending to 3.5 percent of gross domestic product, according to reports on September 15 that cited government sources. The figure would match a benchmark adopted by NATO members and pledged by South Korea, and it marks a further step away from the roughly 1 percent ceiling Japan observed for decades. The move follows sustained pressure from Washington on allies to shoulder more of their own defense, and it comes after Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi accelerated spending toward 2 percent of GDP ahead of schedule.

The reports moved markets. On September 21, yields on Japan's 30-year government bonds reached a three-decade high, a sign that investors are weighing how a sustained increase in military outlays would be financed in a country carrying one of the world's largest public debt burdens.

One Perspective

Supporters of a larger budget argue that Japan faces a harsher security environment than at any point since the Cold War. They point to China's rapid military expansion, North Korea's missile development, and increased Russian activity in the Pacific as reasons to build a better-funded and more capable force. In this view, a credible Japanese contribution strengthens the alliance with the United States, reinforces deterrence across the region, and reduces the risk that allies are seen as unwilling to defend themselves. Washington has explicitly urged Tokyo to reduce its reliance on American forces.

Another Perspective

Critics, including some economists and, implicitly, the bond market, warn that Japan's fiscal position makes a rapid increase difficult to sustain without higher taxes, deeper borrowing, or cuts elsewhere. The jump in long-term yields reflects that unease. Beijing and some voices in the region frame the plan as fuel for a regional arms race and as a further erosion of the pacifist posture Japan adopted after 1945. They argue that a spending target set to match allies risks prioritizing a political signal over a clear assessment of what the money would actually buy.

The Strategic Reality

The 3.5 percent figure echoes benchmarks that NATO members have moved toward and that Seoul has pledged, reflecting a broader shift among United States allies toward higher defense budgets. Analysts note that a target phased in over a decade leaves considerable room for adjustment, and that Japan's Article 9 constitutional constraints and coalition politics will shape what is ultimately spent and on what. The market reaction underscores the central tension of the moment. Allied capitals are being asked to expand military capacity at the same time that debt levels and interest costs are rising, and the balance they strike will shape both regional deterrence and their own fiscal stability. Reporting on the plan appeared in the Taipei Times and Bloomberg.

DIPLOMACY · UNITED NATIONS

The UN General Assembly Opens Its High-Level Week

Est. read time: 3 min · Global · Multilateral Diplomacy

World leaders are gathering in New York for the United Nations General Assembly's high-level week, with the general debate of the 81st session set to begin on September 22 under the theme "Restoring trust, managing transformation." It is the final session to open under Secretary-General Antonio Guterres, whose second and final term ends on December 31, 2026. Wars in Ukraine, Gaza, and Sudan, along with questions over the organization's funding and its capacity to reform, are expected to dominate the week.

One Perspective

Defenders of the institution see the Assembly as the one forum where nearly every state speaks as a formal equal, which they consider essential for coordinating responses to conflict, climate change, and humanitarian emergencies. They point to the diplomatic momentum around recognition of a Palestinian state over the past year as evidence that the body can still shape international norms, and they argue that a leaders' meeting sustains channels of communication that quieter periods erode.

Another Perspective

Skeptics counter that the United Nations, and in particular a Security Council bound by the veto, has been unable to halt the largest conflicts of the moment. They argue that set-piece speeches rarely change facts on the ground, and that a looming budget shortfall, with the United States and other large contributors reconsidering their payments, exposes a structural weakness. In this reading, the annual gathering risks becoming a stage for grievance rather than a mechanism for resolution.

The Strategic Reality

General Assembly resolutions are non-binding, and the body's influence is normative rather than coercive, which means its power depends on how far states choose to act on the positions taken there. Efforts to reform the Security Council, including proposals to expand membership or limit the veto, have stalled for decades. A transition at the top of the Secretariat and a tightening budget make this session a practical test of the organization's relevance in a more fragmented international order. Details on the session are set out by the United Nations, with preview coverage from CNN.

SECURITY · SAHEL

JNIM's Fuel Blockade Tightens Its Siege on Mali's Capital

Est. read time: 4 min · Global South · Security

A fuel blockade imposed by the al-Qaeda-linked group Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin, known as JNIM, has choked Mali's capital, Bamako, for roughly a year. The group intercepts and burns tanker convoys along the routes that carry the fuel Mali cannot produce, and open-source trackers report that more than 300 tankers have been destroyed since the blockade began in September 2025. The army now escorts fuel into the city in armed convoys, and a September 17 attack on military installations in Bamako underscored how exposed the capital remains.

One Perspective

Mali's military government, which has drawn closer to Russia for security support, frames the confrontation as a war it can win through force. Officials emphasize that armed convoys are keeping fuel moving into Bamako, and they rule out negotiations with a group they regard as a terrorist organization. In this view, concessions would reward violence and cede leverage, and the answer is to press the fight and protect the supply corridors rather than to bargain over them.

Another Perspective

Analysts and humanitarian organizations argue that the blockade is succeeding as a form of economic warfare. They describe fuel rationing that prioritizes military and government use, shuttered businesses, long queues, and a capital dimmed at night by reduced power generation. In this reading, the junta's reliance on force and on foreign fighters has not reopened the roads, and the strategy is deepening civilian hardship while leaving the underlying siege intact. Some contend that only a broader political approach can ease the pressure.

The Strategic Reality

JNIM's choice to strangle supply lines rather than seize cities reflects a wider shift toward economic coercion that governments find difficult and costly to counter. The crisis compounds instability across the Sahel following the withdrawal of Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso from the regional bloc ECOWAS and their pivot toward Moscow. Analysts warn that a prolonged siege risks spillover toward the coastal states of West Africa, and that the fuel war has become a measure of whether the region's military governments can deliver the security they promised. Tracking of the blockade is maintained by ACLED and Bellingcat, with regional analysis from the Critical Threats Project.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What does it mean for a country to spend a certain percentage of GDP on defense, and why is a figure like 2 or 3.5 percent used as a benchmark?

A: Defense spending measured as a share of gross domestic product compares military outlays to the overall size of a country's economy, which allows rough comparison across states of very different sizes. NATO long treated 2 percent as a target and has since moved toward higher figures. The number functions as much as a political signal of commitment as a precise measure of capability, because how a budget is spent matters as much as how large it is.

Q: What is the difference between the UN General Assembly and the UN Security Council?

A: The General Assembly includes all member states, each with a single vote, and it passes non-binding resolutions across a broad range of issues. The Security Council has 15 members, including five permanent members that hold veto power, and it can adopt binding measures such as sanctions or the authorization of force. The veto is the main reason the Council is often deadlocked on the largest conflicts.

Q: How can an armed group pressure a country without capturing its cities?

A: A group can exert pressure by controlling supply routes, borders, and economic chokepoints rather than by holding territory. Cutting off fuel, food, or trade can strain a government and its population without a direct assault, a form of economic or siege warfare that is difficult and expensive for states to break. The approach trades the visible control of ground for leverage over daily life.
