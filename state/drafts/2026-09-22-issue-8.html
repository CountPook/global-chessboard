COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Washington Expands Its Military Role in Greenland in a Deal With Denmark

Est. read time: 4 min · Arctic · Alliances

On September 18, the United States, Denmark, and Greenland announced a security agreement that gives Washington an expanded military presence and a larger security role on the Arctic island, while leaving sovereignty untouched. President Trump described the accord as granting the United States permanent control over Greenland's security, while Danish and Greenlandic leaders stressed that the island remains part of Denmark and that Greenlanders keep the right to self determination. The deal follows months of quiet negotiation and a period of open friction, after Trump repeatedly suggested the United States should acquire the territory. Officials say the agreement, an update to a 1951 defense arrangement, is expected to be signed at the United Nations General Assembly in New York and still requires approval by the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments. It matters because it settles, at least for now, a dispute that had strained relations among three NATO partners and drawn attention to the Arctic's growing strategic weight.

One Perspective

Supporters in Washington and Copenhagen present the agreement as a pragmatic way to defuse a damaging quarrel while addressing real security concerns. The Arctic is becoming more accessible as ice recedes, Russian and Chinese activity in the region has grown, and Greenland sits along approaches that matter for missile warning and North Atlantic defense. From this view, formalizing an expanded American presence within the existing Danish framework strengthens NATO's northern flank without forcing Denmark to cede land or Greenlanders to surrender their political future.

Another Perspective

Critics, including many Greenlanders, view the process with unease. Polls have shown strong opposition to American annexation, and the largest protests in the island's history followed Trump's earlier statements about taking control. Skeptics question what permanent control over security means in practice, worry that a population of around 56,000 has limited leverage against a great power, and argue that the language of the deal leaves room for an American footprint that could grow over time. For them, the agreement eases an immediate crisis without resolving deeper questions about consent and autonomy.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the United States has kept a military presence in Greenland since the Second World War and that the 1951 arrangement already allowed American bases. The new deal formalizes and broadens that role at a moment when the Arctic has climbed the list of strategic priorities for Washington, Moscow, and Beijing. Much will depend on the text that is signed and on ratification by the Danish and Greenlandic legislatures, which retain the ability to shape or constrain how the agreement is carried out. Whether it stands as a durable settlement or a pause in a longer contest will become clearer as those steps unfold.

ECONOMICS · SANCTIONS

A New US Law Gives Washington Power to Tax Buyers of Russian Oil

Est. read time: 4 min · Russia · Trade

On September 18, President Trump signed a sweeping sanctions law targeting Russia and Iran, named for Senator Lindsey Graham, one of its chief sponsors. The measure gives the president authority to impose tariffs of up to 100 percent on goods from countries that buy Russian crude oil or natural gas, and it tightens sanctions on Russian officials, financial institutions, defense related networks, and the so called shadow fleet of tankers used to move oil around Western restrictions. It also extends the Iran Sanctions Act for another five years. The bill cleared the Senate by 86 votes to 11 in August and the House by 262 votes to 159 in September before reaching the president's desk. Its significance lies in the leverage it hands Washington over the countries, chiefly India and China, that have become the largest buyers of discounted Russian energy since 2022.

One Perspective

Backers argue the law is a needed escalation of economic pressure aimed at draining the revenue that funds Russia's war in Ukraine. They contend that earlier sanctions left large gaps, particularly the shadow fleet and continued purchases by major Asian economies, and that the threat of steep tariffs on those buyers could finally raise the cost of financing Moscow's war effort. In this view, giving the president discretion over whether to apply the tariffs preserves room to negotiate while keeping a credible instrument in reserve.

Another Perspective

Skeptics warn that secondary tariffs are a blunt instrument that could strain relations with India and China, disrupt global energy markets, and raise prices for consumers far beyond Russia. Some note that the president keeps wide discretion over whether and when to act, which makes the law's real effect uncertain and dependent on political calculation. Others caution that aggressive use of tariffs against large economies could speed up efforts to trade outside the dollar and Western financial systems, blunting the long term power of sanctions themselves.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the law as a significant expansion of the tools available to Washington rather than an automatic change in policy. Whether it reshapes Russian revenue depends on how, and whether, the president chooses to use it, and on how buyers such as India and China respond. Past sanctions have shown both real bite and a persistent capacity for evasion, and Russia has spent years building workarounds through alternative shipping, insurance, and payment channels. The measure adds pressure and uncertainty to already tense relationships, but its practical impact will be measured over months, in enforcement decisions and in the reactions of the world's largest energy buyers.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

Saudi Defenses Intercept a Houthi Missile Aimed at Riyadh

Est. read time: 3 min · Gulf · Security

Before dawn on September 19, Saudi air defenses intercepted and destroyed a ballistic missile fired toward Riyadh by Yemen's Houthi movement, according to the Saudi led coalition, which reported no casualties. Residents described hearing an explosion, and the capital's main airport saw brief disruption. A Houthi military spokesman said the group had launched ballistic and cruise missiles and drones at what it called sensitive sites in Riyadh and at Aramco facilities near Yanbu. It was the first reported attempt to strike the Saudi capital since a recent escalation, and it revived memories of the sustained missile and drone campaign the Houthis waged against the kingdom during the war in Yemen.

One Perspective

Riyadh and its partners frame the strike as evidence that the Houthis remain a persistent threat to civilian areas and critical energy infrastructure, and that continued investment in layered air defense is justified. From this position, the interception shows both the danger posed by the group's expanding arsenal, much of it linked to Iranian technology, and the effectiveness of the defenses that protected a city of millions. Saudi officials point to the targeting of an airport and energy sites as proof that the group aims at the economy and at ordinary people.

Another Perspective

The Houthis and their supporters cast the launch as a response to what they describe as aggression against Yemen and against allies in the region, and as a demonstration that they can still reach deep into Saudi territory despite years of war and blockade. In this framing, long range strikes are a form of deterrence and leverage for a movement that controls much of northern Yemen and sees itself as part of a wider regional axis. They argue that the kingdom's own military operations and the broader conflict are the source of the escalation.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that Houthi missile and drone capability has grown substantially over the past decade, giving the group the ability to threaten targets hundreds of kilometers away and to complicate security across the Arabian Peninsula and the Red Sea. A truce framework had reduced direct attacks on Saudi cities for a period, so a strike on Riyadh signals a meaningful escalation with implications for oil markets, shipping, and regional diplomacy. Much will depend on whether this was an isolated event or the start of a renewed campaign, and on how Riyadh, Tehran, and outside powers calibrate their responses in the days ahead.

Sources: France 24, Al Jazeera, Al Arabiya.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What makes the Arctic so strategically important now?

A: As sea ice retreats, the Arctic is becoming more navigable, opening shipping routes and access to oil, gas, and mineral deposits that were once hard to reach. The region also sits along the shortest flight paths between North America and Eurasia, which gives it value for missile early warning and defense. Those factors have drawn growing attention and investment from the United States, Russia, and China, turning a once peripheral region into an arena of great power competition.

Q: What is a shadow fleet, and why does it matter for sanctions?

A: A shadow fleet is the collection of aging, often opaquely owned tankers that move oil for sanctioned sellers while avoiding Western shipping, insurance, and financial services. By operating outside those systems, the vessels help countries like Russia and Iran keep exporting despite restrictions. Targeting the fleet is an attempt to close a major gap in sanctions enforcement, though the ships are hard to track and the networks behind them adapt quickly.

Q: Do secondary sanctions and tariffs on third countries actually work?

A: Their record is mixed. Secondary measures, which penalize other countries for doing business with a sanctioned state, can raise the cost of that trade and press governments to change course. But large economies with leverage of their own may resist, seek alternative arrangements, or absorb the cost, and heavy handed use can strain alliances and encourage parallel financial systems. Effectiveness usually depends on how widely the measures are enforced and how united the countries imposing them remain.
