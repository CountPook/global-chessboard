US Peace Envoys Shuttle Between Moscow and Kyiv

Est. read time: 4 min · Eastern Europe · Diplomacy

On September 5 the United States envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow, then traveled to Kyiv the next day for talks with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Both governments agreed to hold off on aerial strikes against each other's capitals while the envoys traveled, producing a brief lull but not a wider ceasefire. The shuttle is the most active round of American mediation in months, and it comes with the front lines largely frozen and both sides bracing for another winter of fighting. Its significance lies in whether Washington can turn episodic contacts into a durable framework, or whether the effort stalls as earlier attempts did.

One Perspective

Kyiv and its European backers argue that any settlement must rest on firm security guarantees rather than promises, and that a ceasefire cannot reward territorial conquest. Zelenskyy has said he expects the fighting to continue and is counting on continued American support through the winter, while pressing for Western commitments that would deter a future Russian offensive. From this position, freezing the war along the current line without guarantees would leave Ukraine exposed and validate the use of force to redraw borders.

Another Perspective

Moscow maintains that a lasting settlement requires Ukraine to cede the Donbas, a region Russia only partly controls, and to give up its ambition to join NATO. Russian officials frame these as the root causes of the conflict rather than bargaining chips, and they have rejected a ceasefire along the existing front as a device that would let Ukraine rearm. In this reading, the terms put to the American envoys are the baseline for peace, not an opening bid.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the talks remain far from a breakthrough. The core dispute, over territory and Ukraine's future alignment, is the same one that has blocked every previous round, and the pause on strikes against capitals was a courtesy for the visit rather than a step toward a truce. American officials have signaled they hope to revive three way talks later in September, after Russia's parliamentary elections and before the United States midterm campaign intensifies, a calendar that leaves a narrow window. Earlier mediation efforts have foundered on the gap between a ceasefire in place, which favors the side holding ground, and a full settlement with enforceable guarantees, and that gap remains wide.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NPR, CNN.

POLITICS · EUROPE

Germany's Far Right Wins a State Election for the First Time

Est. read time: 4 min · Germany · Elections

The Alternative for Germany, known as the AfD, finished first in the September 6 state election in Saxony-Anhalt with about 43.8 percent of the vote, the first time the party has topped the poll in any German state election. The result more than doubled its 2021 showing and pushed the Christian Democrats, who had led every government in the state since 2002, down to roughly 17 percent. The AfD fell about three seats short of an outright majority in the 83 seat legislature, and because every other party has ruled out governing with it, forming any administration will require an unwieldy alliance of rivals. The vote matters as the clearest sign yet of the far right's growing strength in the formerly communist east and a test of the mainstream's strategy of exclusion.

One Perspective

Supporters cast the result as a democratic verdict that the established parties can no longer ignore, driven by anger over immigration, energy costs, and a sense that eastern Germany has been overlooked for decades. They argue that refusing to let the largest party govern, or even negotiate, is itself undemocratic and will only deepen voters' alienation. From this view the informal firewall against the AfD becomes hard to sustain when the party is winning close to a plurality of the electorate.

Another Perspective

Germany's mainstream parties and many civil society groups counter that the AfD, parts of which the domestic intelligence service has classified as extremist, poses a threat to the constitutional order that justifies keeping it from power. They contend that cooperating with the party would normalize positions on migration, national history, and the European Union that cross democratic red lines. In this framing an awkward coalition of opponents is a price worth paying to deny the far right control of a state government.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts see the outcome as part of a broader pattern across Europe, where nationalist and anti establishment parties have gained ground amid economic strain and disputes over migration. The immediate question is governability, since the only majority available runs from the center right through the Greens and the Left, a combination with little in common beyond opposition to the AfD. The result also raises the stakes for federal politics and for the party's momentum ahead of future national contests. Whether exclusion contains the AfD or fuels it is a debate now playing out in real time, with little clear precedent to guide it.

Sources: France 24, NPR, Al Jazeera.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

An Israeli Strike Tests a Fraying Gaza Ceasefire

Est. read time: 3 min · Middle East · Security

An Israeli airstrike in Gaza City on September 7 killed a Hamas commander, with the Israeli military saying the man had been directing attacks on its forces and rebuilding the group's capabilities in violation of the truce. The strike is the latest breach of a fragile ceasefire that has held in broad terms since October 2025 under an American backed phased peace plan, even as intermittent fire continues. Israel controls a little more than half of the territory behind a demarcation known as the yellow line, while Hamas retains a hold on the rest, and humanitarian access remains far below what the plan promised. The episode underscores how far the arrangement sits from a stable peace.

One Perspective

Israel argues that the ceasefire permits it to act against imminent threats and that targeted strikes on militants who are regrouping amount to enforcement rather than violation. Officials point to Israeli soldiers killed since the truce began as evidence that Hamas has not laid down its arms, and they insist the group must be prevented from rearming or reasserting control. From this position the airstrike is a measured response to a party that is itself breaking the terms.

Another Perspective

Palestinian officials and Hamas counter that repeated Israeli strikes, raids beyond the agreed line, and restrictions on aid amount to a steady erosion of the ceasefire that inflicts continued civilian harm. They argue that the flow of humanitarian supplies, well short of the levels the plan envisioned, shows Israel is not honoring its own commitments. In this reading the truce exists mainly on paper while the population endures ongoing deprivation and violence.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the current phase as a managed conflict rather than a resolved one. The peace plan moved Hamas to give up its formal civil administration earlier in 2026, but no agreement has settled the harder questions of who governs Gaza, how it is rebuilt, and what becomes of the remaining armed factions. Aid monitors report that only a fraction of the allocated relief convoys have entered, deepening a humanitarian crisis, and each strike and counterstrike tests whether the framework can hold. Without progress on governance and reconstruction, observers warn, the ceasefire risks remaining a pause rather than an end to the war.

Sources: The Times of Israel, Al Jazeera.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What are security guarantees, and why are they such a sticking point in ending the war in Ukraine?

A: Security guarantees are commitments by outside powers to help defend a country if it is attacked again, ranging from promises of weapons and intelligence to, at the strongest end, a pledge to intervene militarily. Kyiv wants guarantees credible enough to deter a future offensive, since a ceasefire alone would freeze the current front without preventing another war. The difficulty is that the strongest guarantees risk drawing guarantor states directly into conflict with a nuclear armed Russia, which is why governments have been cautious about how far to commit.

Q: Why do many mainstream European parties refuse to govern with the far right even when it wins the most votes?

A: The practice, often called a cordon sanitaire or firewall, reflects a judgment that some parties hold positions incompatible with liberal democratic norms on matters such as migration, minority rights, or the rule of law. Supporters of the approach argue it protects institutions from being reshaped by forces hostile to them, while critics say excluding a party that large frustrates voters and can strengthen its appeal. The tension between respecting an election result and defending constitutional guardrails sits at the heart of the debate.

Q: What does it actually mean for a ceasefire to hold when fighting still occurs?

A: A ceasefire is an agreement to stop combat, but in practice many are partial or fragile, with sporadic strikes, raids, and accusations of violation continuing on both sides. Analysts often distinguish between a halt to large scale hostilities and a genuine political settlement that resolves the underlying dispute. A truce can sharply reduce violence and open space for negotiations while still falling well short of peace, which is why observers watch whether the parties build on it or let it unravel.
