COVER STORY · ISSUE #4

Washington and Ottawa Race a Tariff Deadline as Fifty Percent Duties Loom

Est. read time: 4 min · Trade · North America

On July 20 President Donald Trump signed proclamations imposing an additional 50 percent tariff, effective August 19, on a fresh basket of Canadian goods that includes certain dairy products, alcoholic beverages, and motor vehicles, covering roughly 20 billion dollars of imports. Ottawa and Washington are now in an intensive phase of talks aimed at an interim deal before the deadline. On August 6 Prime Minister Mark Carney said the negotiations had turned "nasty" after Trump criticized Canada and its leadership, while insisting Canada remained at the table to protect its workers and businesses.

The stakes are amplified by how tightly the two economies are woven together. The Ambassador Bridge alone handles a large share of the goods that cross the border each day, and a sustained tariff wall would ripple through supply chains on both sides.

One Perspective

Washington casts the tariffs as leverage against what it calls long-standing irritants. Its central complaint is Canada's dairy supply management system, which uses production quotas, set pricing, and import limits on dairy, eggs, and poultry. US officials argue the system unfairly shuts out American farmers who want to sell north of the border. The administration also pressed Canada to drop its digital services tax, which Ottawa agreed to rescind, and to ease procurement rules that favor Canadian suppliers. In this framing, tariffs are a tool to force a rebalancing of a relationship the White House sees as tilted.

Another Perspective

Ottawa presents the same measures as coercion aimed at a partner that has already given ground. Carney frames the talks as a fight to shield Canadian workers, and his government notes it withdrew the digital tax in the hope of a broader arrangement. Canadian dairy farmers have warned the government not to trade away supply management, which they see as the backbone of rural farm incomes. Canada has also signaled it is prepared to counter US auto tariffs, arguing that concessions cannot be one directional if the deal is to hold at home.

The Strategic Reality

Trade analysts describe the arrangement under discussion as a first phase, an interim deal that would pause the sharpest tariffs while larger questions are pushed into the fall. The new 50 percent basket touches only a slice of total Canadian exports, but the political symbolism is large, and it lands against the backdrop of the coming review of the USMCA continental trade pact. Because the two economies share integrated auto, energy, and agricultural chains, the cost of a prolonged standoff falls on producers and consumers in both countries, which is precisely why a narrow, face-saving agreement remains the most likely path even as the rhetoric hardens.

SECURITY · EUROPE AND NORTHEAST ASIA

North Korea Sends a Missile Unit Into Russia as the Alliance Deepens

Est. read time: 4 min · Security · Russia and North Korea

Ukraine's military intelligence directorate said this week that a North Korean missile unit has begun deploying to western Russia. According to the Ukrainian assessment reported on August 5 and 6, the unit could be equipped with as many as 120 ballistic missiles and six launchers, with roughly 90 North Korean personnel folded into Russia's 112th Missile Brigade in the Voronezh region. The disclosure follows what Kyiv describes as Russia's renewed use of North Korean ballistic missiles against Ukrainian targets. Russia's defense ministry and North Korea's mission to the United Nations did not respond to requests for comment.

If confirmed, the move would mark a shift from the infantry that Pyongyang has stationed on Russian soil since late 2025 toward a dedicated strike capability, a further tightening of a partnership formalized in a 2024 treaty.

One Perspective

Kyiv and its Western partners read the deployment as a dangerous escalation. In this view, North Korea is moving from supplying shells and manpower to operating weapons that can reach Ukrainian cities, while gaining battlefield experience, hard currency, and access to Russian military technology in return. Analysts note that North Korea's KN-23 and KN-24 missiles carry heavier payloads than comparable Russian systems but are far less accurate, a combination that has been linked to strikes with high civilian casualties. For Ukraine, the arrival of a foreign missile brigade turns a bilateral war into a test of a wider authoritarian alignment.

Another Perspective

Moscow and Pyongyang have not confirmed the deployment, and both frame their cooperation as the lawful product of a mutual defense treaty between sovereign states. From that vantage point, the partnership mirrors the flow of Western weapons, funding, and intelligence into Ukraine, and the two governments argue they are entitled to arrange their own security relationships without external veto. The absence of an official response leaves the specifics resting largely on Ukrainian intelligence, which has an interest in spotlighting the depth of the Russia and North Korea axis.

The Strategic Reality

Independent analysts caution that the report comes from a party to the conflict and has not been externally verified, even as the broader trajectory is well documented. North Korea has already sent millions of artillery shells and, by allied estimates, thousands of troops to support Russia since 2024. The significance is less any single shipment than the normalization of a security bridge between Europe and Northeast Asia, one that hands Pyongyang a live proving ground and revenue while eroding the sanctions meant to isolate it. Seoul and Tokyo are watching closely, because whatever North Korea learns in Russia it can carry home.

INDO-PACIFIC · SOUTH CHINA SEA

Manila Files a UN Map of Scarborough Shoal and Beijing Answers With Drills

Est. read time: 3 min · Maritime Law · South China Sea

On July 29 the Philippines deposited with the United Nations an official nautical chart of Scarborough Shoal, known in Manila as Bajo de Masinloc and in Beijing as Huangyan Dao. The filing was made under Article 16 of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, which asks coastal states to give public notice of the baselines from which their maritime zones are measured. The chart sets out the shoal's normal baselines and the outer limits of a 12 nautical mile territorial sea and a 24 nautical mile contiguous zone. China rejected the step as illegal and void, and on August 1 the People's Liberation Army conducted naval and air drills near the feature. Taiwan, which also claims the shoal, restated its own position.

One Perspective

Manila presents the filing as a measured, lawful act of transparency. Officials describe it as consistent with UNCLOS and with the 2016 arbitral ruling that rejected the bulk of China's expansive claims, and as a way to assert sovereign rights through paperwork and international institutions rather than confrontation at sea. After repeated clashes around the shoal, the Philippine government argues that putting its claim on the formal record strengthens its hand without firing a shot.

Another Perspective

Beijing insists the shoal is Chinese territory and says the Philippine filing changes nothing. Its foreign ministry called the move illegal and void, arguing that the scope of Philippine territory was fixed by earlier treaties that never included the shoal, and it warned of a resolute response to what it labels provocation. In China's telling, the UN deposit is a unilateral attempt to dress a contested claim in legal clothing, and the drills near the shoal are a demonstration that control on the water, not filings on paper, will decide the question.

The Strategic Reality

Scarborough Shoal sits at the intersection of law and force. Three governments claim it, the 2016 tribunal in The Hague favored Manila, and Beijing rejects that ruling outright, so the dispute plays out through a mix of legal filings and gray zone pressure rather than a decisive contest. The pattern matters because the Philippines is bound to the United States by a mutual defense treaty, which raises the cost of any miscalculation, while a regional code of conduct between China and Southeast Asian states remains unfinished after years of talks. Each paper claim and each patrol is a move to shape the baseline from which the next round is argued.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What is supply management, and why is it so central to the Canada and US dispute?

A: Supply management is the Canadian system that controls how much dairy, eggs, and poultry farmers produce, sets prices for those goods, and limits imports through quotas. It is meant to stabilize farm incomes and shield producers from price swings. Washington sees the import limits as a barrier that keeps American products out, which is why it has become a headline demand in the tariff talks, and why Canadian farmers treat any concession as an existential threat.

Q: Why would North Korea send weapons and troops to a war in Europe?

A: The relationship is transactional. In exchange for shells, missiles, and manpower, Pyongyang is widely assessed to receive hard currency, food, fuel, and access to advanced military technology, along with a rare chance to test its weapons and rotate soldiers through real combat. It also cements a strategic alignment with Moscow at a moment when both governments are under heavy Western sanctions, giving each a partner that helps blunt that isolation.

Q: What does it mean to deposit baselines with the UN, and does that settle who owns a reef?

A: Under the Law of the Sea, states are expected to publish the baselines they use to measure their territorial waters and other zones, and depositing a chart with the UN puts that claim on the international record. It is a notice, not a verdict. The filing signals how a country intends to draw its maritime rights, but it does not by itself resolve a competing sovereignty claim, and enforcement on the water remains a separate contest entirely.
