COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Yemeni Forces Retake the Bab-el-Mandeb as the Mecca Alliance Activates

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Security

On October 5 Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan agreed at an emergency meeting in Riyadh to activate the collective deterrence provisions of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement for the first time since the pact was signed in August, clearing the way for member forces and capabilities to deploy inside Saudi Arabia. The move came as Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces said they had pushed Iran-aligned Houthi fighters back from the Bab-el-Mandeb, the narrow strait at the southern end of the Red Sea, after the Houthis seized the port of Mocha and threatened the chokepoint in September. Roughly a tenth of the world's seaborne trade, along with a large share of oil and gas shipments, passes through the strait, which makes control of its approaches a matter of international concern. The combination of a newly invoked alliance and a shifting front in Yemen raises the stakes around one of the most important maritime passages on the map.

One Perspective

The Yemeni government and its backers present the offensive as the recovery of national territory from an armed group that has repeatedly attacked commercial shipping and draws support from Iran. From this view, pushing the Houthis away from the Bab-el-Mandeb protects the freedom of navigation that the wider world relies on, and invoking the Mecca pact signals that Riyadh and its partners will treat threats to the strait and to Saudi territory as a shared security problem rather than a local one. Supporters argue that a credible deterrent is the only way to keep the waterway open and to prevent a single faction from holding global trade at risk.

Another Perspective

The Houthis and their supporters frame their advance as resistance to foreign intervention and to a government they describe as externally imposed, and they tie their Red Sea operations to the war in Gaza and to pressure on Israel. Critics of the alliance response warn that formalizing a multinational deployment in Saudi Arabia could deepen a regional confrontation with Iran, pull more outside militaries into Yemen's long civil war, and harden divisions rather than steady the strait. They point out that earlier coalition campaigns in Yemen produced years of stalemate and severe humanitarian suffering without a decisive result.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts caution that activating a mutual defense pact is not the same as fielding an integrated force, and that turning the Mecca agreement into joint command, basing, and sustained operations would take time and expose political differences among its members. Control of the Bab-el-Mandeb has shifted before, and holding ground near the strait is harder than seizing it. The broader pattern many observers point to is the return of the Red Sea as a contested waterway, where Yemen's internal war, Gulf security politics, and the shadow of the Iran and Israel rivalry now intersect. Whether the alliance's first activation produces a durable change or a brief show of resolve will depend on what the signatories actually do in the weeks ahead.

SECURITY · EUROPE

Germany Arrests a Former Spy Chief on Suspicion of Treason

Est. read time: 4 min · Europe · Intelligence

German federal prosecutors announced on October 6 that they had arrested August Hanning, who led the country's foreign intelligence service, the Bundesnachrichtendienst or BND, from 1998 to 2005, on suspicion of treasonous espionage and the attempted betrayal of state secrets. Prosecutors allege that several years after leaving office he arranged, through a former senior BND official, to obtain classified material and used it to prepare an analysis for a foreign intelligence service, which they did not name. A second man described as a former BND chief of staff, who remained in government service until earlier this year, was also charged with aiding the scheme. The case is striking because it reaches into the leadership of an agency built to guard secrets, and officials have described it as one of the most serious suspected breaches in years.

One Perspective

For Germany's security establishment, the arrest shows that the rule of law applies even to those who once ran the state's most sensitive institutions, and that counterintelligence is doing its job. Officials who take this view argue that acting on the evidence, however senior the suspect, is essential to deterrence and to the confidence of allies who share intelligence with Berlin. They stress that the charges are allegations to be tested in court, and that the willingness to pursue a former spy chief reflects institutional strength rather than weakness.

Another Perspective

Others urge caution against drawing sweeping conclusions before the facts are established, noting that Hanning has not been convicted and that the identity of the foreign service involved has not been disclosed. Some former intelligence figures and commentators warn that a prosecution of this profile could be read abroad as a sign of disarray in German intelligence, and they question how a retired official could reach current secrets years after leaving his post. From this angle the case raises as many questions about oversight and internal security as it answers, and its full meaning will not be clear until the evidence is public.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts place the case in a wider context of intensified espionage across Europe since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, a period in which several European states have arrested suspected agents and expelled diplomats. Germany, as a large economy, a NATO member, and a leading supporter of Ukraine, is a priority target for foreign intelligence collection. Historically, breaches at the top of an intelligence service have done lasting damage to morale and to relationships with partner agencies, and they often trigger internal reviews of how access to secrets is controlled. The practical consequences here will hinge on what was taken, who received it, and how far the alleged arrangement extended.

POLITICS · AMERICAS

Brazil's First Round Delivers a Surprise and Sets Up an October Runoff

Est. read time: 3 min · Latin America · Elections

Brazil's presidential election produced a first round on October 4 that upended expectations, with Flavio Bolsonaro, a senator and son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, taking the most votes and forcing a runoff against the incumbent, President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva. Reported results gave Bolsonaro about 47 percent and Lula roughly 45 percent, a lead that most pre-election polling had not foreseen. Together the two camps drew more than nine in ten votes, underscoring how firmly Brazilian politics remains split between them. Financial markets reacted quickly, with Brazilian stocks and the real rising as investors anticipated a more market-friendly result and a stronger conservative presence in Congress. The two candidates will meet again in a second round set for October 25.

One Perspective

Supporters of Bolsonaro read the result as a rebuke of the incumbent on crime, the economy, and the cost of living, and as momentum for a conservative program of lower taxes, lighter regulation, and a tougher line on public security. They point to the market rally as a sign that investors welcome the prospect of a change in direction, and they argue that a conservative majority in Congress would make the country easier to govern along those lines. In this reading the first round reflects a genuine shift in the public mood rather than a protest vote.

Another Perspective

Lula's camp and its allies counter that first-round numbers do not decide the contest and that the president retains a path to victory by consolidating support on the left and center before the runoff. They warn that a Bolsonaro presidency would put social programs, environmental protections in the Amazon, and institutions they say were strained under the previous administration at risk. From this perspective the vote is a close and unsettled race rather than a realigning verdict, and the second round will turn on turnout and on voters who backed the candidates now eliminated.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that Brazil, the largest economy in Latin America and a leading voice in the Global South, carries weight on trade, climate, and the balance between Western and non-Western blocs, so the outcome matters well beyond its borders. Runoffs in Brazil have historically tightened as the field narrows and both camps court undecided voters, which makes a gap of about two points far from decisive. Markets can move sharply on expectations and reverse just as fast if the race shifts. The country's foreign policy posture, including its role in the BRICS grouping and its approach to major conflicts, could look different depending on who prevails, which is why capitals and investors alike will watch the second round closely.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why is the Bab-el-Mandeb strait so important to the global economy?

A: It is one of a handful of maritime chokepoints that world trade depends on. The strait connects the Red Sea and the Suez Canal route to the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, and a large share of the oil, gas, and container traffic moving between Europe and Asia passes through it. When the strait is threatened, shippers often reroute around the southern tip of Africa, which adds time and cost and ripples through global prices.

Q: What is the difference between treason and espionage in law?

A: The two often overlap but are not identical. Espionage generally refers to obtaining or passing protected information to a foreign power, while treason is a broader betrayal of one's own state that, in many legal systems, includes acting to help a foreign government against national interests. Exact definitions vary by country, and prosecutors frequently bring related charges together, as in the German case, because a single course of conduct can meet more than one standard.

Q: Why do many countries hold a runoff instead of deciding an election in one round?

A: A runoff, or second round, is used in many presidential systems to ensure the winner has majority support rather than only a plurality. If no candidate clears 50 percent in the first round, the top two advance to a second vote, which pushes them to broaden their appeal to supporters of the eliminated candidates. The design aims to give the eventual president a clearer mandate, though it can also lengthen campaigns and sharpen polarization.
