COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

US Envoys Shuttle Between Moscow and Kyiv to Revive Ukraine Talks

Est. read time: 4 min · Diplomacy · Russia-Ukraine

Over the first weekend of September, US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner carried a Trump administration proposal for ending the war in Ukraine from one capital to the other. They spent more than three hours with President Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on September 5, then flew to Kyiv and met President Volodymyr Zelenskyy on September 6, the pair's first visit to the Ukrainian capital since the full scale invasion began. Both delegations described the talks as substantive, and Ukraine called for a return to a three way negotiating format alongside the United States and Russia, though no breakthrough was announced. The shuttle matters because it is the most direct American attempt in months to bridge the gap between Moscow and Kyiv on the questions that have blocked every prior round, territory and security guarantees.

One Perspective

Kyiv and its European backers argue that any settlement has to rest on firm security guarantees and cannot reward the seizure of territory by force. Ukrainian officials used the meetings to press for air defense missiles before another winter of strikes on the power grid, and for written commitments that would deter a future attack. From this position, a ceasefire that merely freezes the current front line without binding guarantees would leave Ukraine exposed and give Moscow time to rebuild, so the substance of the guarantees matters far more than the speed of a deal.

Another Perspective

Moscow presents itself as open to a negotiated end to the war but has shown little sign of softening its core demands, which include recognition of its territorial gains and limits on Ukraine's military and its alignment with the West. Russian officials called the Kremlin talks constructive and said the conversation extended to possible economic cooperation with the United States, framing a settlement as part of a broader reset with Washington rather than a concession to Kyiv. In this telling, it is Ukraine and its European supporters who must lower their expectations for the terms to align.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts caution that the return of high level American shuttle diplomacy signals intent more than imminent agreement. Previous trilateral rounds have repeatedly foundered on the same two issues, the status of occupied territory and the strength of any guarantee for Ukraine, and neither capital has yet moved on them in public. The timing, before another hard winter and with Ukrainian air defenses stretched, gives Washington leverage to push for at least a partial pause in strikes. History suggests that wars of this scale tend to end through exhausting negotiation rather than a single meeting, and the near term test of this effort will be whether the two sides agree to sit at the same table at all.

Sources: CNN, RFE/RL, NPR.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

Israeli Strikes on Southern Lebanon Test a Fragile Truce

Est. read time: 4 min · Lebanon · Ceasefire

Israeli air strikes hit several towns in the Nabatieh district of southern Lebanon at dawn on September 6, killing at least four people by the Lebanese health ministry's count, with some local outlets reporting a higher toll. One strike destroyed the Ghandour hospital, the first time a hospital in the area had been leveled in the current phase of the conflict, and another damaged a government building. Israel said the raids answered an alleged Hezbollah drone launch toward its forces in the zone it still occupies, an attack it said caused no casualties. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun appealed to Washington and the wider international community to help halt the strikes, which landed despite a ceasefire and a framework deal meant to wind the conflict down.

One Perspective

Israel maintains that it will keep striking to prevent Hezbollah from rebuilding its arsenal and its positions near the border, and it casts each raid as a targeted response to a specific threat. Israeli officials argue that the ceasefire and the later framework both hinge on the disarmament of the group, and that continued drone activity and the movement of fighters justify military pressure. From this view, holding fire would allow Hezbollah to reconstitute the capability that triggered the last war.

Another Perspective

Lebanese officials and Hezbollah counter that Israel is the party breaking the truce, pointing to a long tally of strikes that have killed civilians and hit medical and municipal sites while Israeli troops remain on Lebanese soil. Hezbollah has rejected calls to disarm before a full Israeli withdrawal, describing a sequence that ties its weapons to that withdrawal as a dangerous trap. Beirut argues that the strikes undercut the credibility of the very agreement they are said to enforce and make it harder for the Lebanese state to extend its own authority across the south.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the ceasefire first signed in late 2024 has been breached many times and that a framework reached in Washington in June set out only a sequenced and lightly enforced path toward disarmament and withdrawal. The core problem is one of sequencing and trust, each side insists the other move first, and neither the Lebanese army nor outside monitors have the leverage to compel compliance. Without a mechanism that verifies steps on both sides at once, the pattern of drone launches followed by heavier retaliation is likely to persist, keeping a formally paused war in a state of slow escalation.

Sources: France 24, Al Jazeera, L'Orient Today.

STRATEGY · GLOBAL ORDER

Xi Takes to the Road to Pitch China as a Stable Alternative

Est. read time: 3 min · China · Global South

Chinese President Xi Jinping has embarked on an unusually crowded stretch of diplomacy, a run of state visits and summits that his government is using to present China as a steady partner at a time it describes as defined by American unpredictability. The sequence began with a state visit to Kyrgyzstan and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit in early September, and is expected to run through further stops before a state visit to Washington and a meeting with President Trump on September 24. Xi has sharply limited his foreign travel in recent years, so the packed schedule is itself a signal of Beijing's priorities. The effort matters because it lays out, in one compressed period, China's bid to court the Global South and to shape the terms of its most important relationship before sitting down with Washington.

One Perspective

Beijing frames the tour as proof that China offers developing nations a reliable and predictable alternative to a United States seen as transactional under Trump. Chinese officials point to trade, infrastructure financing, and a defense of multilateral institutions as evidence that Beijing wants to reinforce a rules based order rather than upend it, and they note that a majority of the world's people live in the Global South that China is courting. From this position, expanding partnerships strengthens Beijing's hand and gives smaller states room to hedge between the two largest powers.

Another Perspective

Skeptics in Washington and among some of China's neighbors read the same trips as an effort to peel partners away from the United States and to build a bloc that can dilute Western influence in bodies like the United Nations. They point to unresolved frictions over trade practices, technology, and territorial claims, and question how stable a partner Beijing really is for countries that have their own disputes with China. In this reading, the language of stability is a diplomatic instrument aimed at reshaping the international order in China's favor rather than defending the existing one.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the moment as a contest over which power looks more dependable to the rest of the world. China's advantage lies in showing up with sustained attention and economic offers, while its constraints include slower growth at home and lingering wariness among neighbors over its conduct in disputed waters. The planned Washington summit will test whether the two governments can manage trade, technology, Taiwan, and other flashpoints without an open rupture. Whatever its outcome, the flurry of visits underscores a longer trend, a more assertive Chinese diplomacy competing directly for influence across the developing world.

Sources: The Washington Post, CNBC, The Diplomat.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What is a trilateral negotiation, and why is the format itself so contested?

A: A trilateral negotiation brings three parties into the same process, here the United States as mediator alongside Russia and Ukraine. The format matters because it decides who is in the room and whose demands are treated as central. Warring sides often resist meeting directly, so shuttle diplomacy, in which a mediator carries messages between separate capitals, can be a step toward a joint table. Agreeing to sit together is frequently a substantive concession in its own right, not merely a logistical detail.

Q: Why do ceasefires so often fail to stop the fighting?

A: A ceasefire is only as durable as the mechanisms that verify it and the willingness of each side to comply. Many rest on ambiguous language, lack neutral monitors with real authority, and leave core disputes unresolved, which lets each party accuse the other of breaking the terms. When one side believes the other is using the pause to rearm, or when troops remain on contested ground, the incentive to resume limited strikes can overwhelm a paper agreement.

Q: What do analysts mean by the Global South, and why are major powers competing for it?

A: The Global South is a loose term for the developing countries of Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East, home to most of the world's population. These states hold votes in international institutions, sit astride vital resources and trade routes, and represent fast growing markets. As competition between the United States and China sharpens, both powers court them with investment, security ties, and diplomatic attention, and many of the countries themselves work to avoid choosing a side.
