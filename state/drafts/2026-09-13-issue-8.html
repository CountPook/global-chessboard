COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

BRICS Adopts the New Delhi Declaration as India Presses a Global South Agenda

Est. read time: 4 min · South Asia · Multipolar Order

On September 12 the leaders of the BRICS group adopted the New Delhi Declaration at the bloc's summit, hosted by India under a chairship theme of resilience, innovation, cooperation, and sustainability. Prime Minister Narendra Modi said the text passed unanimously with no member raising an objection, a notable result given sharp divisions among members over conflicts in West Asia. The declaration emphasized local-currency trade, cross-border payment systems, cooperation on artificial intelligence, energy security, and a stronger collective voice for the Global South, while stopping short of any common currency. It matters as a test of whether an enlarged and diverse BRICS can still speak with one voice as it grows.

One Perspective

India and the bloc's supporters frame the declaration as evidence that BRICS is becoming a durable platform for the Global South and an alternative center of gravity to Western-led institutions. New Delhi points to its mediation between the United Arab Emirates and Iran to secure a joint statement, and to unanimous adoption despite divisions over West Asia, as proof of its convening power and the group's continued relevance. In this view, local-currency trade and shared payment systems are practical steps to reduce dependence on the dollar and to insulate members from the reach of Western sanctions.

Another Perspective

Skeptics argue the declaration is long on aspiration and short on binding commitments. An enlarged BRICS that spans both rivals and partners of the West struggles to agree on specifics, and the retreat from a common currency shows the limits of efforts to move away from the dollar. Watered-down language on the most contentious conflicts, they say, reflects a lowest-common-denominator consensus rather than a coherent alternative order, and the group's expansion may dilute rather than concentrate its influence.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that BRICS now accounts for a large share of the world's population and a growing share of global output, but that its members' divergent interests constrain collective action. The rivalry between India and China, and the members' varied relationships with Washington, make binding commitments difficult, and declaratory summitry has historically outpaced institution-building. The push toward local-currency settlement and new payment rails is real and incremental, driven partly by exposure to sanctions, yet a genuine parallel financial system remains distant. The summit's clearest achievement may be diplomatic, keeping a fractious and expanding group in the same room and on the same page for a day.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

Houthis Complete a Takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Maritime Security

On September 11 and 12 Yemen's Houthis captured a strategic island in the Bab el-Mandeb strait and seized stretches of Red Sea coastline, consolidating control over one of the world's busiest shipping chokepoints, the corridor that links the Red Sea and the Suez route to the Gulf of Aden. The advance followed the movement's heaviest missile and drone assault in years on Saudi Arabia, which set oil sites ablaze and wounded dozens, and prompted Riyadh to shut down its East-West pipeline as a precaution. The United Nations migration agency said the wider offensive has killed more than 500 people and displaced tens of thousands. It matters because control of the strait hands the Houthis leverage over trade between Europe and Asia and raises the risk of a broader regional war.

One Perspective

Saudi Arabia and Western governments see the takeover as an Iranian-enabled threat to freedom of navigation and to Gulf security. The United States president said Iran was probably behind an aerial attack on Saudi Arabia, and Saudi officials pointed to drones launched from Iraq striking the East-West pipeline. From this position, an armed movement seizing a global chokepoint, with backing from Tehran, is an intolerable danger to commerce and to the kingdom's security, one that may require a firm military response to reverse.

Another Perspective

The Houthis present their advance as a legitimate extension of control over Yemeni territory and a response to years of war and blockade, and by the United States president's account they have signaled that they do not want Washington drawn directly into the fighting. Tehran denies directing the group and rejects responsibility for the strikes on Saudi Arabia. In this framing the movement is a domestic actor consolidating gains in a long civil war, not an Iranian proxy seizing a global waterway on Tehran's behalf.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that Bab el-Mandeb carries a significant share of the world's seaborne trade and energy, so disruption there quickly reverberates through insurance costs, rerouting around southern Africa, and global supply chains. The chokepoint has been a flashpoint since Houthi attacks on shipping escalated after 2023. Whether the group can hold the island and coastline it has taken, and whether Saudi Arabia or a United States-led coalition moves to intervene, will determine if this is a temporary escalation or a lasting shift in who controls the strait. The precautionary pipeline shutdown is a reminder of how fast regional violence can reach energy markets.

SECURITY · EUROPE

NATO Scrambles Jets After Russian Drones Cross Into Poland

Est. read time: 3 min · Europe · NATO and Russia

On September 10 roughly two dozen Russian drones crossed into Polish airspace during a large overnight assault on Ukraine, prompting Warsaw to halt operations at four airports and NATO to deploy aircraft and ground-based air defenses. Polish and allied forces shot down a number of the drones, among the first times NATO members have fired on Russian assets over alliance territory during the war. NATO allies condemned the incursions as dangerous and unacceptable and said Russia bears full responsibility, while Moscow's account of the incident differed. It matters because it brings the war's violence directly onto NATO soil and tests how the alliance responds.

One Perspective

Warsaw and its allies see the incursions as a deliberate probe of NATO's defenses and resolve, part of a pattern of airspace violations that has also affected Romania. From this position, downing the drones was a necessary and proportionate defense of sovereign territory, and the episode justifies reinforcing the alliance's eastern flank and building stronger integrated air and drone defenses. Allowing repeated violations to go unanswered, they argue, would only invite further escalation.

Another Perspective

Moscow has generally denied intentionally targeting NATO territory and has cast such incidents as accidents or the result of Ukrainian air defenses, while warning that deeper Western involvement risks direct confrontation. President Vladimir Putin, speaking in the same period, said that deploying European troops to Ukraine would mean war with Russia, even as he insisted Moscow harbors no aggressive intent toward Europe. In this framing the danger comes from Western escalation rather than from Russian strikes near the border.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that drones straying across borders are an inherent hazard of a war fought along NATO's edge, but that a pattern of incursions erodes the line between accident and provocation. The episode revives debate over how the alliance should answer incidents that fall below the threshold of an Article 5 attack, and over the cost of using expensive interceptors against cheap drones. The central question is whether NATO can deter further violations without being pulled into the direct clash that both sides say they want to avoid.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What makes a maritime chokepoint like Bab el-Mandeb so strategically important?

A: A chokepoint is a narrow passage that a large volume of trade must transit, so whoever controls it gains outsized leverage over global commerce. The Bab el-Mandeb links the Red Sea and the Suez route to the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, carrying a substantial share of seaborne oil and container traffic between Europe and Asia. Disruption forces ships to reroute around southern Africa, adding time, fuel, and insurance costs that ripple through supply chains far from the strait itself.

Q: What does it mean to trade in local currencies, and why are some countries pursuing it?

A: Local-currency trade means two countries settle transactions in their own currencies rather than converting through a third currency such as the United States dollar. Supporters argue it reduces exposure to exchange-rate swings and to sanctions that can cut off access to dollar-based payment systems. In practice it faces real hurdles, including currency volatility, trade imbalances that leave one side holding money it cannot easily spend, and the deep liquidity and trust that keep the dollar dominant in global finance.

Q: When drones cross into NATO airspace, what determines whether it triggers a collective response?

A: NATO's founding treaty separates consultation from collective defense. Article 4 lets any member call for talks when it feels its security is threatened, while Article 5 treats an armed attack on one member as an attack on all. Whether an incident rises to the level of Article 5 depends on judgments about intent, scale, and damage, and allies generally treat stray or unattributed drones as matters for consultation and national defense rather than for automatic collective military action.
