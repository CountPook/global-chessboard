COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

The United States Completes Its Military Withdrawal From Iraq

Est. read time: 4 min · Iraq · Security

The United States announced on September 30 that it had completed the withdrawal of its remaining troops from Iraq, ending a military presence that began in 2014 as part of the campaign against the Islamic State group. Commanders said the last American forces and equipment departed through Erbil Air Base, closing out a mission that Washington and Baghdad had agreed in 2024 to wind down. Officials said the United States would continue to provide targeted training and intelligence support to Iraqi forces, a sign that the relationship is shifting rather than ending. The move matters because it removes a direct American military footprint from a country that sits at the fault line between United States and Iranian influence in the region.

One Perspective

Supporters of the withdrawal, including much of Iraq's political leadership, frame it as the natural end of a mission that had achieved its core aim. The Islamic State no longer holds territory, Iraqi security forces have been rebuilt over a decade of training, and many Iraqi politicians had long argued that a foreign troop presence was an affront to national sovereignty and a magnet for armed factions. In this view the drawdown fulfills a commitment made to Baghdad and lets Iraq define its own security on its own terms, backed by continued cooperation rather than boots on the ground.

Another Perspective

Critics warn that the timing is risky. Iraqi officials have pointed to renewed Islamic State activity across the border in Syria and cautioned that fighters could exploit a thinner security presence to regroup. Others argue that a full American exit reduces Washington's leverage over Iran aligned militias operating inside Iraq and could leave Baghdad more dependent on Tehran. For this camp the lesson of the past is that a hurried departure can create the vacuum that extremist movements need, and that partnership from a distance is not the same as presence.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that this is not the first time American forces have left Iraq. A withdrawal completed in 2011 was followed three years later by the rapid rise of the Islamic State, a sequence that shapes how both capitals approach the current transition. The difference now is that Iraqi forces are more experienced and that Washington is retaining an advisory and intelligence role rather than departing entirely. Much will depend on whether that residual support is enough to blunt any resurgence, on the stability of the government in Baghdad, and on how the regional contest between the United States and Iran plays out in the space the troops leave behind.

ECONOMICS · EUROPE

Europe Weighs Whether to Tap Frozen Russian Assets for Ukraine

Est. read time: 4 min · European Union · Economics

European Union governments are moving toward a decision on whether to use roughly 210 billion euros in frozen Russian central bank assets to help finance Ukraine. Most of the money is immobilized at Euroclear, a securities depository based in Belgium, where it has sat since the bloc froze it after Russia's 2022 invasion. Foreign ministers are due to take up a fresh sanctions package in Luxembourg on October 12, and leaders are expected to debate the assets at a European Council summit in Brussels on October 15 and 16. The question has gained urgency because Kyiv says it faces a funding gap of around 78 billion dollars in 2027 and existing European commitments are running short.

One Perspective

Governments pushing to use the assets, among them several northern and Baltic states, argue that the country responsible for the war should bear its costs. They contend that Ukraine's defense and eventual reconstruction cannot rest indefinitely on European taxpayers, and that leaving the money idle while Kyiv struggles makes little sense. Some favor a structure often described as a reparations loan, in which the frozen funds would back financing for Ukraine without an outright confiscation, a design meant to answer legal objections while still putting Russian money to work.

Another Perspective

More cautious members, including Belgium, which hosts the bulk of the holdings, along with others wary of the precedent, warn of serious risks. They point to questions of sovereign immunity under international law, to the danger that seizing another state's reserves could unsettle confidence in the euro and in Europe as a place to hold assets, and to the possibility of Russian retaliation against European property. Belgium in particular has stressed that any liability should be shared across the bloc rather than fall on the country where the money happens to sit.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts draw a distinction that sits at the center of the debate, between spending the profits that the frozen assets generate, which the bloc has already begun to channel to Ukraine, and touching the underlying principal, which is far more contentious. Doing the latter would likely require broad agreement among member states and close coordination with other Group of Seven economies to avoid fracturing the response. The decision carries weight beyond Ukraine, since it would set a reference point for how democracies treat the reserves of a state they consider an aggressor, and the outcome in October will signal how much financial risk Europe is willing to absorb to sustain Kyiv.

SECURITY · INDO-PACIFIC

North Korea Tests an Intermediate Range Missile It Says Uses AI

Est. read time: 3 min · Korean Peninsula · Security

North Korea said it had tested an intermediate range missile, with state media reporting on October 4 that the launch took place a day earlier from the east of the country and struck a target around 1,000 kilometers out in the sea off its eastern coast. Pyongyang described the weapon as part of a hypersonic strategic system and claimed it incorporated artificial intelligence, and it said leader Kim Jong Un oversaw the drill. The test came amid rising friction on the peninsula following a landmine blast in the demilitarized zone last month that wounded South Korean soldiers, and after Pyongyang rejected Seoul's demand for an apology. It matters as the latest signal that North Korea intends to keep expanding and advertising its arsenal despite stalled diplomacy.

One Perspective

Pyongyang presents such tests as a sovereign right and a necessary deterrent. North Korean officials routinely frame their weapons programs as a response to the military exercises that the United States and South Korea conduct and to what they describe as a persistent hostile posture on the peninsula. From this standpoint the launch is defensive, a demonstration meant to raise the cost of any attack and to assert that the country will not negotiate its security away under pressure.

Another Perspective

Seoul, Washington, and Tokyo view the tests as destabilizing and as a breach of United Nations Security Council resolutions that bar North Korea's ballistic missile activity. They argue that each launch advances capabilities that threaten neighbors and complicate regional missile defense, and that claims of hypersonic performance and artificial intelligence, if real, would sharpen those concerns. For this group the pattern calls for sustained pressure and coordination rather than concessions, and the timing amid the demilitarized zone tensions makes the launch look like deliberate escalation.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts urge caution on Pyongyang's own descriptions, noting that North Korean claims about advanced features such as hypersonic flight and artificial intelligence often outrun what can be independently verified. The broader pattern is familiar, a steady tempo of tests that serve to refine hardware, rally domestic audiences, and build leverage for any future bargaining. The context has shifted in recent years as North Korea has deepened its ties with Russia, which has eased some of the pressure that sanctions once applied. With talks dormant, the near term trajectory points to more tests rather than fewer, and to a continued debate in Seoul about how to balance deterrence with the search for stability.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: When a country withdraws its troops but keeps a training and intelligence role, what does that actually mean?

A: It usually means the departing power ends its combat and garrison presence while keeping a smaller footprint of advisers, trainers, and intelligence sharing. The host country regains full control of operations and basing, but the partner continues to help with planning, surveillance, and the equipping of local forces. The arrangement lets a government claim it has ended an occupation while preserving influence and situational awareness, though it also means the partner has far less ability to act directly if the security situation deteriorates.

Q: What is the difference between freezing a central bank's assets and seizing them?

A: Freezing, or immobilizing, means the assets stay under their owner's name but cannot be moved or used while sanctions are in force, a reversible step taken often and quickly. Seizing, or confiscating, means transferring ownership outright, which is far rarer and legally fraught because a central bank's reserves have traditionally enjoyed sovereign immunity. The gap between the two explains why governments can quickly freeze funds yet argue for years about whether they can ever be spent.

Q: Why do states carry out missile tests they know will draw condemnation?

A: Tests serve several purposes at once. They validate hardware and let engineers gather data, they signal resolve to adversaries and reassure domestic audiences, and they can build leverage ahead of any negotiation by demonstrating a capability that others must now account for. The international criticism that follows is often part of the calculation rather than a deterrent, since the testing state judges the strategic and political payoff to be worth the diplomatic cost.
