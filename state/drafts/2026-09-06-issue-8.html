COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

US Envoys Meet Putin in Moscow in a New Push to End the Ukraine War

Est. read time: 4 min · Europe · Diplomacy

On September 5 United States envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met President Vladimir Putin in Moscow for roughly three hours of talks, followed by dinner, in an effort to revive negotiations to end the war in Ukraine after nearly eight months of deadlock. Both sides agreed to a three day pause on strikes against each other's capitals, which Putin said would begin at midnight Moscow time, and the envoys were due to travel to Kyiv on September 6 to brief President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. A senior Kremlin aide described the discussions as substantive and constructive, though no breakthrough was announced. The meeting matters as the most direct United States brokered contact between Moscow and Kyiv in months and an early test of whether a wider ceasefire is within reach.

One Perspective

Moscow enters the talks insisting that any settlement address what it calls the root causes of the war, which for the Kremlin means a neutral Ukraine outside NATO and acceptance of the territorial changes Russian forces have imposed on the ground. Russian officials framed the temporary pause and the hospitality shown to the envoys as evidence of goodwill, while holding firm that Kyiv must accept limits on its future alignment in exchange for peace. From this vantage, a durable deal is possible only if the West acknowledges Russia's stated security demands rather than treating the war as a simple case of aggression to be reversed.

Another Perspective

Kyiv argues that a just peace cannot come at the cost of its sovereignty or its ability to defend itself. Zelenskyy has pressed for firm security guarantees, resisted formal recognition of occupied land as Russian, and warned that a pause Moscow could exploit to rest and rearm is not the same as a settlement. Ukraine and several European governments worry that a deal negotiated over their heads would reward the use of force and leave Ukraine exposed to another attack once the fighting stops. In this view, the terms of any guarantee matter as much as the ceasefire itself.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts caution that the gap between the two positions remains wide and that earlier rounds of shuttle diplomacy have faltered on the same questions of territory and guarantees. A short pause on strikes is far easier to arrange than a lasting ceasefire, let alone a political settlement, and each side has reason to appear cooperative without conceding its core aims. Much depends on whether Washington can offer Kyiv credible security assurances and whether European capitals, which would help underwrite any guarantees, are drawn into the process. Historical precedent suggests that talks held under battlefield pressure tend to produce pauses more readily than durable peace.

POLITICS · SOUTHEAST ASIA

Philippine Vice President Sara Duterte Posts Bail After an Arrest Order

Est. read time: 4 min · Indo-Pacific · Domestic Politics

On September 5 Philippine Vice President Sara Duterte posted bail of 360,000 pesos, about 5,700 dollars, a day after a court issued an order for her arrest on three counts of grave threats. The charges stem from remarks she made at a 2024 online news conference, in which she said she had arranged for an assassin to kill President Ferdinand Marcos Jr, the first lady, and the then speaker of the House of Representatives if she herself were killed. Duterte, who denies wrongdoing and says she was voicing fear for her own safety rather than making a threat, obtained a court order lifting the warrant after being fingerprinted. The case deepens a bitter feud between the country's two most powerful political families.

One Perspective

Prosecutors and allies of Marcos argue that a public official cannot describe a plot to kill the head of state without facing consequences, and that pursuing the case reflects the equal application of the law. They note that the charges rest on Duterte's own recorded words and contend that holding a sitting vice president to account shows that no office places a person above criminal law. In this framing, the prosecution is a matter of accountability, not politics.

Another Perspective

Duterte and her supporters describe the case as political persecution meant to sideline her before the 2028 presidential election, when Marcos is barred from running again. Her lawyers argue that an impeachable official should not stand criminal trial while in office and have asked the courts to void the charges. They point to the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling that struck down her impeachment as unconstitutional, and cast the grave threats case as a continuation of the same campaign against her by other means.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts see the case as the latest turn in a rupture between the Marcos and Duterte camps, partners in the 2022 election who have since fallen out. A conviction could disqualify Duterte from public office and end her presidential ambitions, giving a personal feud national stakes. The courts must now weigh her legal challenges, and the outcome will test the independence of Philippine institutions at a time of sharp polarization. Analysts note that high profile prosecutions in the country have often become prolonged and difficult to predict.

ECONOMICS · NORTH AMERICA

Bank of Canada Holds Rates as the Tariff War With Washington Deepens

Est. read time: 3 min · North America · Trade

On September 4 the Bank of Canada kept its benchmark interest rate at 2.25 percent, a seventh consecutive pause, even as an escalating trade conflict with the United States clouds the outlook. Washington invoked a rarely used trade statute in late August to place 50 percent duties on a range of Canadian goods, and Ottawa has prepared retaliatory tariffs on more than 20 billion dollars of United States products, due to take effect September 8. President Donald Trump has also threatened steeper levies on autos from the start of 2027. The central bank's caution reflects the difficulty of setting policy when a trade shock pulls growth and inflation in opposite directions.

One Perspective

Supporters of the hold argue that the bank is right to wait until the effects of the tariff war become clearer. Cutting rates into a supply shock that raises prices could add to inflation, they say, while the full damage to exports and employment has yet to show up in the data. On this view, keeping rates steady preserves the room to act decisively once the direction and size of the shock are known, rather than committing early and having to reverse course.

Another Perspective

Critics counter that standing pat risks leaving the bank behind the curve. With tariffs threatening jobs in export industries and growth already softening, they argue that waiting too long could deepen a downturn that lower rates might have cushioned. Some contend that inflation near 3 percent is being driven by trade barriers rather than strong domestic demand, and that treating it as a reason to hold confuses a one time rise in prices with sustained inflation that monetary policy should resist.

The Strategic Reality

Economists note that central banks have few good options when tariffs raise prices and threaten output at the same time, a bind that monetary policy alone cannot resolve. The pause buys time but does nothing to settle the underlying trade dispute, which will be decided in negotiations between Ottawa and Washington rather than in interest rate decisions. Much depends on whether the September retaliation triggers further escalation or opens a path to talks. The episode shows how trade policy has become a central driver of economic uncertainty across North America.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What are security guarantees in a peace deal, and why does Ukraine want them?

A: Security guarantees are commitments by outside powers to help defend a country if it is attacked again, ranging from promises of weapons and intelligence to more binding pledges of direct support. Ukraine seeks them because a ceasefire on its own would leave it facing a larger neighbor that could rearm and strike later. The strength of any guarantee depends on who offers it and how automatic the commitment is, which is why these details are often harder to negotiate than a pause in the fighting.

Q: Can a sitting vice president or head of state be prosecuted while in office?

A: It depends on the country's constitution. Some legal systems grant serving officials temporary immunity, requiring impeachment or the end of their term before a criminal case can proceed, while others allow prosecutions to move forward against officials still in office. The question is often contested in court, as it is in the Philippines, where it turns on how judges interpret the relationship between impeachment and ordinary criminal law.

Q: How do tariffs affect a central bank's interest rate decisions?

A: Tariffs tend to raise the prices of imported goods, which can push inflation up, while also dampening trade and growth, which can push in the other direction. That leaves a central bank caught between raising rates to fight inflation and cutting them to support the economy. Because the two effects unfold on different timelines, policymakers often pause to see which force proves stronger before committing to a direction.
