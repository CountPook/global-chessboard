COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Iran Floats a Seven Day Roadmap to End the War as Washington Balks

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Diplomacy

On September 25 Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi set out a seven day plan to end the war with the United States and Israel, describing it to reporters outside the United Nations Security Council and saying it had been passed to Washington through Qatar. Under the proposal a ceasefire covering Iran and Lebanon would take hold, Washington would lift its naval blockade, waive oil sanctions, and release billions of dollars in frozen Iranian assets, and only on the seventh day would Tehran reopen the Strait of Hormuz, after which comprehensive nuclear talks would begin. President Donald Trump rejected the framework within a day. The exchange matters because the strait, through which close to a fifth of the world's traded oil moved before the war, has become Tehran's principal source of leverage in a conflict now in its eighth month.

One Perspective

Tehran presents the roadmap as a serious, sequenced path out of a war it says it did not want to prolong, one that trades a reopened strait for relief from sanctions, a blockade, and frozen assets it considers unlawful. President Masoud Pezeshkian has said Iran has no trust in the American side and questions the basis on which any understanding could be reached, arguing that Washington has repeatedly shifted its terms. From this position, front loading Western concessions and leaving the strait until the final day is a way to test American intentions while keeping the country's main point of pressure intact until the end.

Another Perspective

Washington views the plan as an attempt to turn a blockade Iran itself created into a bargaining chip and to secure economic relief before addressing the nuclear program at the center of the conflict. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the talks had produced no major movement toward peace and repeated that Iran will not be permitted to acquire a nuclear weapon. In this reading, rewarding the closure of an international waterway would set a damaging precedent, and any agreement must rest on verifiable limits on uranium enrichment rather than a promise of negotiations to come.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the two positions hardened rather than converged over the week, with the nuclear file notably absent from the Iranian roadmap, which dealt mainly with the ceasefire, the strait, the blockade, frozen assets, and attacks on Arab neighbors. Qatar and other Gulf mediators continue to carry messages between the sides, and the closure of Hormuz has kept energy markets uneasy and complicated shipping across the wider Gulf. Past cycles of pressure and diplomacy with Tehran, from the 2015 nuclear accord to its unraveling, suggest that sequencing and verification, more than the headline terms, tend to decide whether such openings hold. For now the war grinds on while each side waits for the other to move first.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Al Jazeera (Trump rejects), Euronews, The Hill.

ECONOMICS · US AND CHINA

Trump and Xi Extend Their Trade Truce With Few Concrete Gains

Est. read time: 3 min · East Asia · Trade

Chinese President Xi Jinping visited Washington from September 23 to 25 for his second summit of the year with President Donald Trump, and the two governments agreed to extend a trade truce due to lapse in November by two months, until January 10. The meetings produced a handful of narrow understandings, including a new agriculture working group set to meet before the year ends, a channel for reporting artificial intelligence incidents with a follow up dialogue by late November, and a Chinese commitment to buy American coal in 2027 and 2028. More contentious issues went unresolved, as Taiwan appeared in neither side's official readout and the White House statement carried no concession on rare earth exports or tariffs. Beijing said afterward that the extension created space to keep talking.

One Perspective

Those who welcome the outcome argue that in a relationship this fraught, keeping tariffs capped and negotiators at the table is itself worth having. A two month extension, a working group on farm goods, and a mechanism to manage artificial intelligence mishaps lower the risk of a sudden escalation and give each side time to test whether the other will follow through. On this view the summit's value lies in stability and predictability for companies and markets rather than in dramatic breakthroughs, which were never realistic in a single meeting.

Another Perspective

Skeptics counter that the meeting delivered spectacle in place of substance and simply postponed the hardest questions. With no movement on rare earth export controls, where China holds decisive leverage over materials vital to electronics and defense, and no resolution on Taiwan or the broader tariff structure, the truce leaves the underlying rivalry intact. From this angle, a series of short extensions risks normalizing a managed standoff without addressing what drives it, and each deadline becomes a fresh chance for the arrangement to break down.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the summit as continuity by other means, a pause that reflects how deeply the two economies are entangled and how costly a full rupture would be for both. China's dominance of rare earth processing and the United States' lead in advanced semiconductors give each side instruments it is reluctant to surrender, which pushes them toward managed friction rather than either decoupling or genuine detente. Whether the new working groups and communication channels mature into durable agreements or lapse at the next deadline will be the real measure, and the January date now marks the next test of the arrangement.

SECURITY · HORN OF AFRICA

Fighting Returns to Tigray and Threatens Ethiopia's Fragile Peace

Est. read time: 4 min · Horn of Africa · Conflict

On September 23 forces aligned with the Tigray People's Liberation Front seized the airports of Mekelle, Shire, and Axum in northern Ethiopia, prompting Ethiopian Airlines to halt flights to the region as fighting spread into the neighboring Afar and Amhara areas. The escalation followed the front's decision to join a new seven group coalition, styled an alliance for survival, that seeks to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government. The clashes effectively break the November 2022 Pretoria agreement, which ended a two year war that by most estimates killed hundreds of thousands of people. The African Union Commission has called for immediate de-escalation and a return to talks.

One Perspective

Tigrayan leaders and their new allies argue that the federal government never fully carried out the Pretoria agreement, leaving contested territories occupied, displaced people unable to return, and promised political arrangements unmet. In their account the offensive is a response to years of unfulfilled commitments and to what they describe as preparations against them, and the broader coalition reflects grievances shared by Amhara and Oromo armed groups toward the prime minister's rule. From this position the fighting is the consequence of a peace deal that was left hollow.

Another Perspective

Addis Ababa casts the airport seizures and the new alliance as an armed bid to overthrow a sovereign government and a violation of the very agreement the Tigrayan side signed. Federal officials point to the detention of government personnel and the disruption of civilian air travel as evidence of aggression, and they frame their response as a defense of national unity and constitutional order. In this telling the coalition, which they say is backed from outside the country, bears responsibility for reigniting a war Ethiopia had appeared to move past.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts and rights groups warn that renewed war in Tigray could prove even more destabilizing than the last, given the web of armed movements now involved and the risk of drawing in Eritrea, which has been accused of backing the alliance. Human Rights Watch has urged all sides to protect civilians, recalling the mass atrocities and famine like conditions of the earlier conflict. The Horn of Africa is already strained by tension between Ethiopia and Eritrea over sea access and by instability in neighboring Sudan, and a wider Ethiopian war would ripple across the region. Whether outside mediation can freeze the fighting before it hardens into full scale conflict is the immediate question.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Human Rights Watch, NPR, France 24.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why is the Strait of Hormuz so important to the global economy?

A: It is the narrow waterway linking the Gulf to the open ocean, and before the current war close to a fifth of the world's traded oil, along with a large share of liquefied natural gas, passed through it. There is no easy overland alternative for most of that volume, so even the threat of closure can push up energy prices and shipping insurance costs worldwide. That is why control of the strait carries strategic weight far beyond its size.

Q: What are rare earth elements and why do they matter in US and China trade?

A: Rare earths are a group of seventeen metals used in magnets, electronics, electric motors, and defense systems, from smartphones to guided weapons. China processes the large majority of the world's supply, which gives it leverage in trade disputes because export limits can disrupt manufacturing elsewhere. Building alternative mining and processing capacity is possible but slow and expensive, which is why the issue keeps returning to the negotiating table.

Q: What was the 2022 Pretoria agreement in Ethiopia?

A: It was the deal signed in November 2022 in Pretoria, South Africa, that ended roughly two years of war between Ethiopia's federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front. It set out a ceasefire, the disarmament of Tigrayan forces, and the restoration of federal authority and services in the region. Implementation stalled over contested areas and displaced populations, and the current fighting has put the agreement in serious jeopardy.
