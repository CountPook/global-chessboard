COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Washington and Tehran Escalate Their War Across the Gulf

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Security

United States Central Command said it destroyed five Iranian oil tankers on September 8, four in the Gulf of Oman and one near Iran's Kharg Island terminal, describing the vessels as tied to the Revolutionary Guard and its sanctioned oil trade. Washington said the strikes answered an attempted Iranian attack on an American warship. Within hours Iran launched about 20 ballistic missiles at the Al Azraq air base in Jordan, which hosts United States forces, and struck at commercial shipping in the region. Jordan said its air defenses intercepted 18 of the missiles, with two landing in unpopulated areas and no casualties reported. The exchange is one of the sharpest turns yet in a war, pitting Iran against the United States and Israel, that is now more than six months old, and it pushed Brent crude above 100 dollars a barrel for the first time in weeks.

One Perspective

Washington casts the strikes as a proportionate response to repeated Iranian attacks on American vessels and on civilian shipping. United States officials argue that the tankers were both a source of the sanctioned oil revenue that funds Iran's military and, in their account, cover for Revolutionary Guard operations at sea, so degrading them raises the cost of further attacks without committing to a ground war. Supporters of this approach contend that leaving strikes on American forces unanswered would invite more of them and threaten freedom of navigation through the Gulf, and that a limited maritime reprisal is meant to deter Tehran rather than to topple it.

Another Perspective

Tehran describes the tanker strikes as piracy against its lawful commerce and a further attempt to strangle its economy, and frames its missile salvo as legitimate retaliation for months of attacks on Iranian territory and assets. Critics of the American approach, including some governments in the region, warn that each round of strikes and counterstrikes widens the war and endangers the roughly one fifth of the world's seaborne oil that passes through the Strait of Hormuz. They note that the fighting is increasingly spilling onto the territory and into the waters of third countries such as Jordan and the Gulf states, drawing them toward a conflict they did not choose.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the two sides have settled into a pattern of calibrated escalation, each trying to inflict cost while signaling that it does not want an all out war, and that such patterns are hard to keep under control. Qatar and other Gulf states have warned of an industrial and environmental catastrophe if strikes on tankers and energy facilities continue, and traders are pricing a growing risk premium into oil. History offers a caution. From the tanker war of the 1980s to more recent Gulf crises, maritime tit for tat has tended to persist for months, and it is a single miscalculation, a downed aircraft, a sunk warship, a strike with mass casualties, that has most often tipped such standoffs into something larger.

POLITICS · EUROPE

The Far Right Finishes First in a German State Election

Est. read time: 3 min · Europe · Elections

In the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, the Alternative for Germany, or AfD, won the September 6 state election with about 43.8 percent of the vote, its strongest regional result to date and a rise of more than 20 points from the previous election in 2021. The party still fell short of a majority in the 83 seat state legislature, taking 39 of the 42 seats needed to govern alone. The Christian Democratic Union of Chancellor Friedrich Merz, which had led the state for more than three decades, fell to roughly 17 percent. It is the first time the AfD, which parts of Germany's domestic intelligence service have classified as a suspected right wing extremist group, has finished first in a state parliament election.

One Perspective

The AfD and its supporters read the result as proof that a large share of voters, especially in the formerly communist east, have lost faith in the mainstream parties over immigration, energy costs, and the wider economy, and want a decisive change of course. They argue that continuing to shut the largest party out of power through a cross party firewall is itself undemocratic, and that doing so only deepens the resentment that has fueled the party's rise.

Another Perspective

Germany's established parties counter that the firewall is a defense of constitutional and democratic norms against a party that security services regard as partly extremist, and that governing with it would legitimize positions hostile to minorities and to the European order. They contend the vote reflects protest and regional grievance more than a durable majority for the AfD's program, and that the remaining parties can still build a coalition among themselves that keeps it out of office.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that Saxony-Anhalt is a small state, but that the scale of the win and the near collapse of the governing CDU make the outcome hard to dismiss as a simple protest. The national firewall has held so far, yet each strong AfD showing complicates coalition arithmetic and sharpens a question with no easy answer, how long mainstream parties can govern while excluding the party that finished first in parts of the country. The result also reaches beyond Germany. The strength of a Euroskeptic party often sympathetic to Moscow, in the European Union's largest economy, feeds a broader argument about the direction of European politics heading into a difficult autumn.

GLOBAL SOUTH · SOUTH ASIA

Nepal Marks One Year Since the Gen Z Uprising

Est. read time: 3 min · South Asia · Governance

On September 8 Nepal observed the first anniversary of the Gen Z protests that toppled the government of Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli in September 2025. Mourners gathered at the country's burned parliament building to remember the 76 people killed during the unrest, which began over a short lived social media ban and grew into a broader revolt against corruption and an entrenched political class. One year on, an interim government led by former chief justice Sushila Karki is steering the country toward fresh elections while facing hard questions about how much has really changed. The anniversary arrived as Nepal reeled from floods and landslides that officials say have killed more than a thousand people in recent weeks.

One Perspective

Supporters of the movement point to real gains. A political old guard that many Nepalis saw as untouchable was forced from power, an interim leadership was drawn from outside the traditional parties, and the government signed a ten point agreement promising reforms on education, health care, and support for the families of those killed. In this view the uprising broke a long culture of impunity and forced Nepal's institutions to answer, for the first time, to a young and connected generation unwilling to accept business as usual.

Another Perspective

Others, including survivors and the families of the dead, argue that the promises have largely gone unmet and that accountability has stalled. A judicial commission recommended action against senior figures, including the former prime minister, but a later review found insufficient evidence to bring criminal charges, and many of the reforms remain on paper. Critics say the same patronage networks endure beneath a new set of faces, and that the government's handling of the recent floods has revived the very complaints about neglect and mismanagement that first drove people into the streets.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts place Nepal within a wider pattern of youth led uprisings across the Global South, from South Asia to Africa, in which a generation shaped by economic frustration and social media has toppled governments but struggled to convert street power into lasting institutional change. Nepal's coming elections will be an early test of whether the movement can turn into durable reform or fade as older parties regroup. The country's path also draws close attention from India and China, rival powers that both court influence in a small but strategically placed Himalayan state.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: Why does the Strait of Hormuz matter so much when conflict flares in the Gulf?

A: The strait is a narrow waterway linking the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, and roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, along with a large share of its liquefied natural gas, passes through it. Because there is no easy alternative route for much of that volume, any threat to shipping there raises fears of a supply disruption and pushes up global energy prices. That is why even limited naval clashes in the area ripple through markets far from the region.

Q: What is Germany's political firewall, and where does it come from?

A: The firewall, known in German as the Brandmauer, is an informal agreement among the mainstream parties not to govern with or depend on the far right Alternative for Germany. It grew out of the country's effort to guard against extremism after its twentieth century history, and in practice it means the other parties form coalitions among themselves to keep the AfD out of power even where it wins the most votes. Supporters call it a democratic safeguard, while critics say excluding a large bloc of voters carries risks of its own.

Q: Why do so many recent uprisings get called Gen Z movements?

A: The label points to protests led largely by young people who grew up with smartphones and social media, which they use to organize quickly, spread information, and coordinate without traditional party structures. Movements described this way, in places such as Nepal, Bangladesh, Kenya, and Sri Lanka, have often been driven by anger over corruption, joblessness, and inequality. Their speed and decentralization can topple a government, but the same lack of formal structure can make it hard to sustain pressure and secure lasting change afterward.

ECONOMICS

INDO-PACIFIC

China has kept up naval and air patrols around Taiwan and continued building on contested reefs in the South China Sea, while Japan and others expand defense spending. Watch for new incidents at sea and for any signs of a possible leaders' meeting between Washington and Beijing later in the autumn.
