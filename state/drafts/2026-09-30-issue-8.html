COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Washington and Beijing Agree to Cut Tariffs on Sixty Billion Dollars of Goods

Est. read time: 4 min · Trade · East Asia

After a three day state visit by President Xi Jinping to Washington in late September, the United States and China released lists of goods on which each side will lower tariffs, covering roughly sixty billion dollars of trade. Under a framework the two governments describe as thirty for thirty, each country will allow about thirty billion dollars of the other's non-sensitive products to enter at reduced rates, and most of the affected goods will move to what is called most favored nation treatment, the standard rate that strips out the country specific duties layered on during years of confrontation. The measures cover American farm exports such as corn, wheat, and frozen meat alongside Chinese consumer goods, while American soybeans, a major crop prized by farmers, were left off the Chinese list. The deal extends a fragile truce in a trade war that has unsettled global supply chains and markets.

One Perspective

Supporters of the agreement present it as evidence that direct engagement between the two leaders can steady the most consequential economic relationship in the world. They argue that rolling country specific tariffs back toward standard rates lowers costs for businesses and consumers on both sides, restores predictability for exporters, and opens the door to further talks on aviation, financial services, and artificial intelligence. For American farm states that have lost sales during the trade war, even a partial reopening of the Chinese market is welcome relief, and for Beijing the deal eases pressure on exporters at a time of slower growth.

Another Perspective

Skeptics counter that the agreement leaves the core disputes untouched and that a few months of tariff relief does little to resolve the structural friction over technology, subsidies, and market access. Critics in Washington warn that trimming tariffs hands Beijing an economic reprieve without firm commitments on the issues that drove the conflict, while the pointed exclusion of soybeans signals that China intends to keep leverage in reserve. Others note that truces of this kind have repeatedly given way to renewed escalation, leaving exporters unsure whether to treat the opening as durable or temporary.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts describe the arrangement as a managed pause rather than a settlement. The tariff cuts are real and will be felt by specific industries, but they sit atop a still elevated baseline of duties and a truce with a fixed expiry, which keeps both governments' options open. The summit also touched on Taiwan, advanced technology, and the war involving Iran, none of which lend themselves to quick bargains, and the underlying contest over who writes the rules of the global economy continues. Whether this round marks the start of a broader stabilization or simply the latest lull will depend on how both sides handle the harder questions the tariff lists set aside.

SECURITY · MIDDLE EAST

American Forces Complete Their Withdrawal From Iraq After Two Decades

Est. read time: 4 min · Middle East · Security

The United States is completing the withdrawal of its remaining forces from Iraq, closing out a military presence that began with the 2003 invasion and stretched across more than two decades. The pullout follows a two stage agreement reached between Washington and Baghdad in 2024, under which the American led coalition ended its combat mission in federal Iraq in 2025 and wound down the support operations that continued against the remnants of the Islamic State into 2026. Iraqi officials announced that the country's territory was free of coalition forces and stressed that national security services were ready to take full responsibility. The departure marks a milestone in a long American effort in which roughly 4,500 American service members died, and it reshapes the balance of influence in a country where the United States and Iran have long competed.

One Perspective

Those who favor the withdrawal argue that the mission which justified an American presence has largely run its course, with the Islamic State reduced from a territorial force to scattered cells. Iraqi leaders who pressed for the pullout frame it as the natural restoration of full sovereignty, and factions aligned with Iran that long demanded an American exit have celebrated it as a political victory. Supporters in Washington add that keeping troops in a country that increasingly wanted them gone exposed American forces to attacks by armed groups while offering diminishing returns, and that Iraq's own security forces are far more capable than they were a decade ago.

Another Perspective

Critics warn that the withdrawal is premature and risks reopening space that both the Islamic State and militias aligned with Iran could exploit. Kurdish and Sunni figures in particular caution that dormant militant cells remain and that the loss of American air support, surveillance, and intelligence will weaken Iraq's ability to respond to a resurgence. Others in the region argue that the exit cedes strategic ground to Tehran, whose networks are deeply embedded in Iraqi politics and security, and they question whether Baghdad can balance those pressures without an outside counterweight.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the Islamic State, while badly degraded, has not been eliminated, and that its history includes a rapid comeback after an earlier American drawdown last decade. At the same time, Iran's influence in Iraq is now exercised more through politics, economics, and allied armed groups than through any need to confront American troops, so the practical effect of the withdrawal is contested. Much will depend on whether Iraqi forces can sustain pressure on remaining militant networks and whether Baghdad can manage the competing demands of Washington, Tehran, and its own factions. The coming months will test whether the handover produces stability or a vacuum.

POLITICS · NORTH AFRICA

Morocco Names Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri as Its First Woman Prime Minister

Est. read time: 3 min · North Africa · Politics

King Mohammed VI of Morocco named Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri as prime minister on September 29, making her the first woman to lead a government in the country's history. A 50 year old lawyer and the mayor of Marrakesh, Mansouri heads the Authenticity and Modernity Party, which won the most seats in the recent parliamentary election, taking 97 of the 395 places in the House of Representatives. Under Morocco's constitution the monarch appoints the head of government from the party that wins the vote, and Mansouri has now been tasked with assembling a governing coalition. She becomes one of only a handful of women to hold the premiership anywhere in the Arab world.

One Perspective

Many Moroccans and observers across the region hail the appointment as a genuine milestone for women's representation in a part of the world where they remain rare at the top of government. Supporters point to Mansouri's record as a mayor of a major city and a former government minister as evidence that she reached the post on experience rather than symbolism, and they argue that her elevation could encourage broader participation by women in public life. For the governing party, the choice signals a modernizing image and a break with the men who have traditionally filled the role.

Another Perspective

Others urge caution about how much will change in practice. Real power in Morocco remains concentrated in the palace, which oversees security, religious affairs, and major strategic decisions, and the elected government operates within limits the monarchy sets. Skeptics note that the head of government must still build a coalition and deliver on economic grievances, from unemployment to the cost of living, that fueled voter frustration, and they warn that a historic first risks becoming a symbol if it is not matched by the authority to act. Some also question whether a single appointment reflects deeper change in a system where women remain underrepresented in parliament.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts place the appointment within a gradual process of political reform that Morocco has pursued since the constitutional changes that followed the 2011 protests across the Arab world, which strengthened the role of the elected government while preserving the monarchy's dominance. Mansouri's success will be judged less by the history she makes than by whether her coalition can address economic pressures and maintain stability in a country that Western and Gulf partners view as an anchor in North Africa. Her tenure will also serve as a test of how much room a head of government has to govern within Morocco's balance of powers.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What does a most favored nation tariff rate actually mean?

A: A most favored nation rate is the standard tariff a country applies to imports from its ordinary trading partners, rather than a special penalty or preference aimed at one nation. When two governments agree to move goods back to most favored nation levels, they are stripping away the extra country specific duties they had imposed on each other and treating those products like normal trade again. It is a way to unwind a trade conflict without necessarily granting either side a preferential advantage.

Q: Why do powerful states withdraw troops from countries where they have fought for years?

A: Withdrawals usually reflect a mix of shifting priorities, domestic politics, and a judgment that the original mission has been accomplished or is no longer worth its cost. Host governments may also demand an exit as an assertion of sovereignty, and prolonged deployments can expose forces to attacks while straining budgets and public support at home. The hard part is timing, because leaving too early can allow defeated adversaries to regroup, while staying too long can deepen resentment and entangle a foreign power in local disputes.

Q: How much authority does a prime minister hold in a constitutional monarchy?

A: It varies widely depending on the constitution and the political tradition of the country. In some constitutional monarchies the monarch is largely ceremonial and the prime minister runs the government, while in others the crown keeps substantial control over security, foreign policy, or religious affairs and the elected head of government operates within those limits. Understanding a prime minister's real power therefore means looking beyond the title to how authority is actually divided between the palace and the elected institutions.
