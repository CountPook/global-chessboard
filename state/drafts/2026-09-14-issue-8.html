COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Houthis Seize Control of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait

Est. read time: 4 min · Maritime Security · Middle East

On September 10 and 11, 2026, Houthi forces captured the Red Sea port of Mokha and then reached Perim Island, the rocky outcrop that divides the Bab el-Mandeb strait into its two shipping lanes, after Yemeni government forces withdrew. The advance gives the Iran-aligned group effective control over one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints, the gateway between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden through which a large share of seaborne oil and container traffic passes. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman spoke with United States President Donald Trump twice to press for military intervention as the group closed in.

The seizure matters because it hands the Houthis, and by extension Tehran, leverage over global trade and energy flows at a passage that has already endured years of attacks on shipping.

One Perspective

The Houthis present their advance as the recovery of Yemeni territory and as a source of pressure that can be applied on behalf of allies in the region. For Tehran and its partners, a grip on the strait is a strategic asset that complicates Western and Gulf freedom of movement and strengthens the group's hand in any future negotiation. From this position, holding both banks of a global chokepoint is proof that years of air strikes failed to break the movement.

Another Perspective

For Saudi Arabia, the wider Gulf, and Western governments, a hostile force astride the Bab el-Mandeb is a direct threat to commercial shipping, naval movement, and energy security. Riyadh has urged Washington to act, warning that unimpeded Houthi control could throttle a lane that carries a significant portion of the world's oil and goods. In this reading the capture is not a local gain but a regional emergency that invites a military response.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts note that the Bab el-Mandeb has long been one of a handful of maritime chokepoints whose disruption ripples through the global economy, alongside the Strait of Hormuz and the Suez Canal. The Houthis have targeted Red Sea shipping repeatedly since 2023, forcing many carriers to reroute around southern Africa at added cost and time. Holding territory on both sides of the strait is a different matter from harassing ships from a distance, and it raises the prospect of sustained interference rather than intermittent strikes. Whether outside powers respond with force, and whether the group chooses to actually close the lane rather than merely threaten it, will determine how far the disruption spreads.

Sources: Euronews, TIME, Foreign Policy

SECURITY · INDO-PACIFIC

China Threatens to Cancel the Trump Xi Summit Over Taiwan Arms Sales

Est. read time: 4 min · United States and China · Taiwan

On September 13, 2026, China signaled through its foreign ministry that it would cancel a planned September 24 White House summit between Xi Jinping and Donald Trump if Washington approves a roughly 14 billion dollar arms package for Taiwan. Congress authorized the package in January, but it awaits the president's signature. Foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun restated that Taiwan is a red line in relations with the United States.

The dispute forces a choice on Trump weeks before November midterm elections, between a high profile meeting with Xi that he has promoted and a weapons sale that Taiwan says it was assured would proceed.

One Perspective

China holds that Taiwan is an internal matter and that United States arms sales to the island violate Beijing's core interests and prior understandings between the two governments. From this position, proceeding with a major weapons package on the eve of a leaders' summit would be a provocation that makes a friendly meeting impossible, and calling off the summit would be a measured response rather than an overreaction.

Another Perspective

Supporters of the sale in Washington and Taipei argue that arming Taiwan is a longstanding United States commitment rooted in law, and that allowing Beijing to veto it by dangling a summit would set a damaging precedent. Taiwan's defense ministry has said it received written assurances as recently as March that the sale was on track, and officials in Taipei worry that delay would signal that the island's security is negotiable. In this view, holding firm preserves credibility with partners across the Indo-Pacific.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts see the standoff as a test of how far each leader will trade near term optics for longer term position. Trump has invested political capital in the summit ahead of the midterms, while Xi has an interest in slowing United States arms flows to the island. Past administrations have generally proceeded with authorized Taiwan sales despite Chinese objections, so backing down now would mark a notable shift. The plausible outcomes range from a quiet delay of the signature to preserve the meeting, to the summit's collapse if neither side yields, with each path carrying costs for the relationship.

DIPLOMACY · NORTH AFRICA

Algeria Severs Diplomatic Relations With the United Arab Emirates

Est. read time: 3 min · North Africa · The Gulf

On September 10, 2026, Algeria announced that it was severing diplomatic relations with the United Arab Emirates, accusing Abu Dhabi of persistent hostile actions and giving the Emirati ambassador 48 hours to leave the country. Algiers said it would also close its airspace to UAE-registered aircraft, with an exemption for existing commercial routes until the end of the year. The rupture follows years of friction over the Western Sahara, rivalries in the Sahel, and opposing stances toward Israel.

The UAE said it hoped the decision would prove temporary. The break matters because it deepens a rift between two influential states in the Arab world and North Africa, with consequences for how other governments in the region align.

One Perspective

Algiers frames the move as a defense of its sovereignty and principles after what it describes as repeated provocations. Algeria backs the Polisario Front in the Western Sahara, opposes normalization with Israel, and views Emirati influence in the Sahel and support for its rival Morocco as threats to its regional standing. From this position, cutting ties is the culmination of exhausted patience rather than an impulsive act.

Another Perspective

Abu Dhabi has expressed regret and a hope that relations can be restored, presenting itself as an aggrieved party seeking calm. The UAE has built a wide network of partnerships across Africa and the Middle East and casts its regional role as investment and stabilization rather than interference. In this framing, the rupture is Algeria's choice, and the door remains open to repair.

The Strategic Reality

Analysts place the break within a broader realignment in which Gulf states have expanded their economic and security reach into North Africa and the Sahel, often on the opposite side from Algeria. The Western Sahara dispute, sharpened by a growing list of countries backing Morocco's autonomy plan, remains a defining fault line for Algiers. Severing ties is a strong signal but a reversible one, and much will depend on whether the two governments allow tempers to cool or let the dispute harden into a lasting estrangement that draws in other regional players.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What makes a maritime chokepoint like the Bab el-Mandeb so important?

A: A chokepoint is a narrow passage that a large volume of shipping must pass through, so disruption there can affect trade and energy prices far beyond the immediate area. The Bab el-Mandeb links the Red Sea and the Suez route to the Indian Ocean, which means interference can force vessels onto much longer voyages around Africa, raising costs and delaying goods. A handful of these passages, including the Strait of Hormuz and the Malacca Strait, carry a disproportionate share of the world's seaborne oil and containers, which gives whoever controls them outsized leverage.

Q: Why do United States arms sales to Taiwan cause such friction with China?

A: Beijing considers Taiwan part of China and treats foreign military support for the island as interference in its internal affairs, while Washington maintains longstanding but carefully worded commitments to help Taiwan defend itself. The result is a recurring clash in which each sale is read in Beijing as a challenge to its sovereignty and in Washington and Taipei as a routine fulfillment of policy. Because both sides attach core interests to the question, arms sales regularly become flashpoints even when the wider relationship is otherwise stable.

Q: What does it mean when two countries cut diplomatic relations?

A: Severing diplomatic relations usually means withdrawing ambassadors, closing or downgrading embassies, and halting official channels of communication, though it stops short of a formal state of war. It is one of the strongest peacetime signals of displeasure a government can send, and it can disrupt trade, travel, and cooperation on shared problems. Such breaks are often reversible, and states frequently restore ties once the underlying grievance eases or a mediator steps in.
