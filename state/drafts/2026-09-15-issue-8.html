COVER STORY · ISSUE #8

Trump Backs a United Ireland and Breaks With Washington Neutrality

Est. read time: 5 min · Diplomacy · Ireland

During a two day visit to Ireland that mixed diplomacy with a golf tournament, United States President Donald Trump said he would love to see the island of Ireland unified, telling reporters in Dublin that reunification was going to happen eventually and describing the prospect as a fantastic thing. Sitting beside Taoiseach Micheal Martin, and after separate talks with Irish President Catherine Connolly, Trump broke with a longstanding convention under which American presidents avoid taking sides on whether Northern Ireland should remain in the United Kingdom or join the Republic.

The remarks landed only weeks after the British government said a border poll was off the table, and they drew swift praise from Irish nationalists and sharp objections from unionists in Belfast and officials in London.

One Perspective

Irish nationalists welcomed the intervention as a signal that the debate over unification is entering the mainstream of international politics. Sinn Fein leader Mary Lou McDonald said the conversation on Irish unity had shifted and urged the Irish government to begin planning in earnest for a future border poll, including the constitutional, economic and health questions that reunification would raise. For this camp, the Good Friday Agreement already provides a peaceful and democratic route to a united Ireland, and a sitting American president acknowledging that possibility lends the cause added legitimacy.

Another Perspective

Unionists and the British government rejected the idea that Ireland's constitutional future is a matter for outside commentary. Democratic Unionist Party leader Gavin Robinson said the question would be decided by the people of Northern Ireland alone, not by voices in London, Dublin or Washington, and some unionists who had praised Trump on other issues accused him of speaking carelessly about a sensitive settlement. British officials, caught off guard, stressed that the principle of consent written into the peace agreement means no change to Northern Ireland's status without majority support there, and that the conditions for calling a referendum have not been met.

The Strategic Reality

Under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, a border poll can be called when the British government's Northern Ireland secretary judges that a majority appears likely to support leaving the United Kingdom, a threshold that current polling does not clearly meet. Successive American presidents have positioned Washington as a guarantor of the peace process rather than an advocate for either outcome, which is why Trump's words carry weight beyond their immediate content. Analysts note that demography in Northern Ireland has shifted gradually, that opinion surveys still show no settled majority for unification, and that any transition would raise difficult questions about identity, public finances and institutions. The practical effect of the visit may be less a change in policy than a reminder of how quickly the constitutional debate can be reopened.

SECURITY · RED SEA

Houthi Forces Seize the Bab al-Mandeb and Tighten Their Grip on Red Sea Trade

Est. read time: 5 min · Security · Maritime

Houthi forces in Yemen completed their advance along the country's Red Sea coastline over the weekend, seizing the port of Mocha and the strategic island of Mayyun, also known as Perim, at the mouth of the Bab al-Mandeb strait. The group now sits only a short distance from the African shore and holds positions overlooking one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints, through which a large share of Europe bound oil, liquefied natural gas and container traffic passes on its way to the Suez Canal.

Iran, which backs the Houthis, hailed the gains, while Gulf states, Western governments and global shipping lines weighed the risk to energy supplies and trade.

One Perspective

Houthi officials insisted that freedom of navigation through the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandeb would remain safe and orderly, framing their control of the coast as a defensive gain rather than a threat to commerce. Iran described the advance as a triumph for its regional allies while maintaining that the Houthis act independently. Supporters of this view argue that the group has repeatedly said it targets only vessels linked to the adversaries it is at war with, and that control of the strait gives Yemen's de facto authorities leverage to press for an end to blockades and to the wider conflict.

Another Perspective

Saudi Arabia, other Gulf producers and Western maritime powers see the same development as a direct danger to the global economy. Since a wider regional war earlier in the year, Riyadh has increased flows through its east to west pipeline to the Red Sea coast, meaning more of its crude now depends on safe passage through Bab al-Mandeb. Shipping companies, already scarred by earlier attacks in the corridor, warn that even the perception of risk can push insurers and carriers to reroute around southern Africa, adding time and cost to global supply chains. For these actors, Houthi control of both sides of the strait extends Iranian influence over a waterway that neither Gulf capitals nor Washington want to see closed.

The Strategic Reality

The Bab al-Mandeb is one of a small number of chokepoints that shape world trade, linking the Red Sea and the Suez route to the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean. A sustained disruption there would ripple through energy markets and container shipping in ways that echo the earlier Red Sea crisis, when many carriers chose the longer Cape of Good Hope route. Naval powers can escort convoys and strike launch sites, but experience in Yemen suggests that controlling territory from the sea or the air is far harder than deterring individual attacks. The central question is not whether the Houthis can threaten the strait, which they now can, but whether they judge that closing it would serve their interests more than keeping trade, and the revenue it brings, flowing.

GLOBAL SOUTH · CLIMATE AND SECURITY

Nepal Carries Its Flood Catastrophe to the United Nations

Est. read time: 5 min · Climate · South Asia

Nepal appealed to the United Nations this week for help meeting the cost of its deadliest disaster in more than a decade, after catastrophic flooding along its northern border with Tibet left more than 1,300 people confirmed dead and several thousand missing. The floods, set off by a glacial collapse and landslides in the high Himalaya, swept through valleys and settlements with little warning and overwhelmed local rescue capacity.

Government officials framed the appeal around a pointed question for the wider world, asking who should pay for a catastrophe that a small mountain nation did little to cause.

One Perspective

Nepal and other climate exposed states argue that disasters of this kind are being made more frequent and more severe by warming driven largely by wealthy, high emitting economies. In this view, the melting of Himalayan glaciers and the growth of unstable glacial lakes are direct consequences of global emissions, and the countries most responsible bear an obligation to fund both emergency relief and long term adaptation. Advocates point to the loss and damage fund agreed at recent United Nations climate talks as the natural channel for such support and say it must be financed quickly and at scale rather than left as a symbolic gesture.

Another Perspective

Donor governments and some development specialists caution that open ended transfers are not a substitute for preparedness on the ground. They note that early warning systems, land use rules that keep people away from flood paths, resilient infrastructure and effective local governance often determine how deadly a given event becomes. From this standpoint, the priority should be targeted investment in disaster risk reduction and in the institutions that carry out evacuations and rebuilding, with international money tied to measurable results, rather than large unconditional payments that are difficult to track and sustain.

The Strategic Reality

The Himalaya feed rivers that hundreds of millions of people across Nepal, India, China and Bangladesh depend on, which makes flood and glacier risk a regional security question as much as a humanitarian one. Scientists have warned for years that glacial lake outburst floods are among the fastest growing hazards in the region, and that monitoring and cross border data sharing remain patchy. Nepal's appeal also lands inside a longer argument about climate finance, in which pledges have often outpaced actual disbursement. Whatever the United Nations decides, the episode underscores how the physical effects of a warming climate are increasingly colliding with the slow machinery of international cooperation and the unresolved question of who ultimately pays.

READER FAQ

Your Questions, Answered

Each issue we answer the most pressing questions from our readers. Submit yours by replying to this email.

Q: What is a border poll, and how would one actually be triggered in Northern Ireland?

A: A border poll is a referendum on whether Northern Ireland should remain in the United Kingdom or join a united Ireland. Under the Good Friday Agreement, the power to call one rests with the British government's Northern Ireland secretary, who is expected to do so if it appears likely that a majority would vote to leave the United Kingdom. There is no fixed formula for measuring that likelihood, which is part of why the question is so contested.

Q: Why does a narrow strait like the Bab al-Mandeb matter so much to the global economy?

A: The Bab al-Mandeb is the southern gateway to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal, the shortest sea route between Europe and Asia. Oil and gas tankers, container ships and bulk carriers all funnel through it, so even a partial disruption can force vessels onto the far longer route around southern Africa. That raises shipping costs, delays deliveries and can push up energy prices well beyond the region itself.

Q: What is the loss and damage fund that keeps coming up after climate disasters?

A: It is a fund agreed at United Nations climate negotiations to help vulnerable countries cope with harm from climate change that cannot be prevented or adapted to, such as destruction from extreme floods and storms. The idea is that wealthier, higher emitting nations contribute money that poorer, more exposed countries can draw on. The recurring debate is over how much is pledged, how quickly it is paid out and what conditions are attached.
